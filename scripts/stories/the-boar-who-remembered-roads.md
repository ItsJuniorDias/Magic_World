## The Gap That Is Not There

The boar came through the hedge at the same place every night, and there was no place in the hedge.

Sabela had checked. She had checked in daylight with her hands, working along the bottom of it from the gatepost to the corner, pushing her arm in up to the shoulder where the blackthorn allowed and taking it out again scratched. The hedge at that point was two hundred years of blackthorn grown into itself, laid twice by somebody who knew how, and packed at the base with bramble that would have stopped a dog.

At about ten past nine, most nights, the boar came through it as though it were a doorway.

She had seen him first in April, from the bathroom window, and had assumed he had come round the end. In May she watched properly, with the light off, and he did not come round the end. He came out of the hedge at the third fence post, walked diagonally across the top of the vegetable garden without touching a single thing in it, went along the wall, and left through the far hedge at a point where there was also no gap.

He was enormous and he was old. The bristles along his back had gone grey at the tips and there was a notch out of his left ear the size of a coin. He never hurried and he never looked at the house.

What bothered her was the diagonal.

If you are an animal crossing a garden, you cross it. You go where it is easy and you leave where you can. He did not do that. He walked a line — a specific line, the same line, so exactly the same that by June there was a faint pale track worn across the top bed where nothing would grow — and the line did not correspond to anything. It went past the shed at a distance of about a metre and a half. It went between the two apple trees and not around them. It crossed the wall at a place where the wall was highest, and he went over it there, heavily, when there was a low bit twelve metres to the left.

Sabela was eleven and she was extremely interested in things that did not make sense, which her mother described as a phase and her grandfather described, more accurately, as a personality.

The interest had a shape, and the shape was always the same. She did not want an explanation. She wanted the thing to go on being odd for as long as possible while she found out more about it, and she had learned by about nine that if you take a strange thing to an adult too early they hand you back an ordinary one and are pleased with themselves.

So she did not tell anybody about the boar for two months.

She started marking the line. She put a stone at the fence post, a stone at the shed, a stone between the apple trees, a stone at the wall.

When she had four stones down she stood at the gatepost and looked along them, and they made a road.

Not a path. A road: straight, purposeful, wide enough for a cart, going from somewhere behind the hedge to somewhere over the wall. It went through the vegetable garden the way a road goes through a field, which is to say without any interest in the field at all.

She went in and asked her grandfather whether there had ever been a road across the top garden.

He was doing the crossword and he did not look up for a while. Then he said: "Which way was it going?"

Which was, she thought afterwards, an extraordinary way to answer a question about whether something exists.

## Following at a Distance

He told her about the mill over three evenings, in pieces, in the order he felt like telling it, which was not the order it had happened.

There had been a mill up the valley. It had ground for a dozen villages. There had been a road to it — a proper one, stone-based, wide enough for two carts to pass at the wide places — and it had come down off the hill, through what was now their top garden, and out over the wall towards the ford.

The mill closed in 1888. The road was formally stopped up in 1890, which is a phrase that means somebody wrote it down somewhere, and after that the hedges were planted across it and the fields were rearranged and within a generation there was no road and within two there was no memory of a road.

"So how do you know about it?"

"Because my grandmother's grandmother carried flour along it," her grandfather said, "and she told my grandmother, and my grandmother told me, and I am telling you, and that is the entire mechanism. There is nothing clever about it."

Sabela followed the boar in July, on a warm night when it did not get properly dark until after a dozen.

She did not follow him closely. She had been told about boars, at length, by three different adults, and she had believed all of them. She went behind at about forty metres, in trainers, on grass, with a torch she did not turn on.

He went through the hedge.

She could not. She had to go round by the gate and along the lane and back up the far side, which took her four minutes, and she was certain she had lost him, and when she came over the rise he was standing in the middle of the Oteros' hayfield waiting.

Not for her. She wants to be clear about that; he never once acknowledged her in the whole business. He was waiting the way a person waits at a level crossing, facing forward, for something to be over.

Then he walked on, and she followed, and this is where it got strange.

He walked across the hayfield in a line. Through the crop, which was standing, which he did not eat. Out through the far hedge, where there was — she checked afterwards, in daylight, with her hands — no gap. Along the edge of a plantation of young pines that had been planted in 2004 and that he went through as though the trees were somewhere else. Down a bank so steep that she had to sit and slide, at a point where the bank had clearly, once, been cut into steps.

By the time they came out at the river she had stopped being frightened and started being something else that she did not have a word for at eleven and has never quite found since.

She was walking on a road.

It was not there. There was bramble and bracken and a wire fence and a plantation and somebody's hayfield. But her feet knew, in the way your feet know a stair in the dark, that the ground she was putting them on had been made.

## The Mill

The mill was less than she expected and much more.

She had imagined a building. What was there, at the bottom of the slope where the river turned, was a rectangle of walls about waist high, full of elder, with one gable end standing to about her shoulder and a doorway in it with the lintel still across the top.

The river went past it fast and shallow and busy, as though it had been let off.

The boar walked into the rectangle through the doorway.

That was the thing that undid her. Not the road, not the hedges, not the walking through pines that were not there when the road was. It was that he had the whole of a wall-less ruin available to him on three sides, and he went in through the door.

He stood in the middle of the floor for a while. Sabela sat down on the wall about six metres away, because her legs had decided.

She had the sense, sitting there in the smell of elder and cold water at half past eleven at night, that she had understood something and would spend a while catching up to it.

He was not lost. That was the first part. Everybody who had ever half-seen him crossing a field at dusk had assumed he was wandering, because that is what a big animal in the wrong place looks like from a car.

He was not wandering. He was commuting.

And the second part, which took her longer, and which she only got hold of properly on the walk home in the dark: the road was still there. It had never gone anywhere. Roads are not made of the surface; the surface is just the part people maintain. The cut into the bank was still cut. The gradient was still graded. Somebody in about 1740 had chosen that line down that hill because it was the line that worked, and the choosing was still true, and the hedges and the pine plantation and the wire fence were all things that had been laid on top of a fact.

The boar was not remembering the road.

The boar was just using it, the way you use a thing that is there, and everybody else had stopped being able to see it.

She got home at ten past one. Her grandfather was in the kitchen with the light on, not making a fuss about it, doing the crossword he had already finished.

"Well?" he said.

"It goes in through the door," said Sabela.

Her grandfather put the pen down and looked at her for a moment, and then he laughed — one short surprised laugh, not at her — and he said, "Does it. Does it now."

## What the Map Said

The archive in the town was open on Tuesdays and Thursdays between ten and one, which she found out by walking there four times.

The woman behind the desk was called Dona Amparo and was not, at first, encouraging. She said the tithe maps were not for handling. She said there was an index. She said what exactly was it that Sabela wanted.

Sabela said she wanted to know if there had been a road across her garden before 1890.

Dona Amparo looked at her for a second and then got up and went into the back room without another word, and came out seven minutes later with a flat grey box.

The map was enormous and the colours in it were still bright. It was dated 1871.

It took them a while to find the house, because the house was smaller on the map than Sabela expected and because the field boundaries were all wrong, which was the point. Dona Amparo found it in the end by working out from the church.

And there it was.

A double line, ruled, going down off the hill from the top left, through the plot that was now their garden, over the corner of what was now the Oteros' hayfield, along the edge of nothing in particular, down the bank at the place with the steps, and along the river to a small black rectangle labelled, in beautiful sloping brown ink, *Muíño*.

Sabela put her finger about a centimetre above the paper and traced it, and it was the line. Every turn. The distance-and-a-half past where the shed would be built in 1963. The high part of the wall, which on the map was not a wall at all but the road's own edge.

"You knew where to look," said Dona Amparo.

"I followed a pig," said Sabela.

There was a pause of the particular kind that happens when an adult decides how to take something.

"Mm," said Dona Amparo, and turned the map round so it faced Sabela properly, and left her with it for the best part of an hour.

The boar still comes through the hedge. He is older now — the notch in his ear has a second notch above it and he takes the bank at the river in two goes instead of one — but he comes at about ten past nine, most nights, and he walks the diagonal.

Sabela has photocopies of the 1871 sheet on her wall and a stone at each of the four points, which her mother has twice tidied away and twice put back after being told.

She has not told anyone at school. She has thought about it and decided that it does not come out well when you say it, and that this is fine, and that the road is not less there for nobody looking at it.

That is, after all, exactly the situation it has been in since 1890.
