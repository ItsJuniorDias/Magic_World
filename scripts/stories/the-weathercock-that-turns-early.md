## A Thing That Turns

Nobody looks at a weathervane, and that is the entire reason this took two hundred years to notice.

A weathervane is designed to turn. That is its only function. It sits on a spindle on a church tower and it turns to face the wind, and if you looked up at it at four o'clock and again at six and it was pointing somewhere else, you would think nothing at all, because that is what it is for.

Femke has tried this on people and it works every time. She asks them which way the vane on their own church was pointing this morning. Nobody has ever known. She asks whether they would notice if it turned while they were watching and they all say yes, and then she asks when they last watched one, and there is a pause.

A weathervane is the most public instrument in a Dutch town and it is the one nobody reads, precisely because everybody has known since they were four exactly what it does.

The cockerel on the Grote Kerk at Nieuwe Sluis is copper, about ninety centimetres from beak to tail, and it went up in 1791 with the tower.

It has been down three times: in 1893 for the spindle, in 1946 for war damage that was not to the cockerel itself but to the mounting, and in 2011 for conservation, when a man from Utrecht took it away for four months and came back with it and a report that is in the church office and that Femke has read.

The report says it is an ordinary copper vane of its period, well made, with a bearing that has been replaced twice, and that it turns freely and is correctly balanced.

Femke's family have been in that town since before the tower. Her great-grandfather was a wheelwright and her grandfather was a wheelwright until there was no such job and then he was in a factory at Zwolle, and the family has lived in three houses on the same street since 1880 and there is nothing remarkable about that in the Netherlands.

The workshop is a hairdresser's now. The yard behind it, where the whole of this starts, is a car park with four spaces, and Femke has stood in it and worked out roughly where a man would have been standing in November 1951 to have that view of the tower over the roofline, and it is between the second and third space.

What is in her family, and what Femke did not know about until she was twelve, is the book.

It is a school exercise book with a marbled cover and it is in a drawer, and it has been added to by four people, and the first entry is in November 1951 in her great-grandfather's handwriting.

Each entry is a date, a time, and two compass bearings.

The first bearing is where it was. The second is where it went to.

That is all. There is no commentary in the first two hundred entries and no explanation anywhere in the book of what it is for, because the man who started it knew what it was for and assumed that anybody holding it would too.

## The Still Thursday

The reason there is a book at all is a Thursday in November 1951.

Her great-grandfather was in the yard behind the workshop at about half past three in the afternoon, and it was flat calm — properly flat, the kind of still that happens over that country in November when a high sits on it, with smoke going straight up from every chimney in the town.

And the cockerel turned.

He watched it go, in still air, through about a hundred and thirty degrees, from south-west round to north-north-west, taking perhaps forty seconds, and stop.

Forty seconds is the detail that Femke keeps coming back to.

A vane in a gust snaps round. It goes in a second or two and it overshoots and comes back and hunts about before it settles, because it is a plate on a spindle being hit by moving air.

Forty seconds, smoothly, through a hundred and thirty degrees, with no overshoot and no hunting, is not what a plate on a spindle does when it is hit by anything. It is what a plate on a spindle does when it is turned.

He went and got two men from the workshop and one of them was the sexton and they stood in the street and looked at it for a quarter of an hour, and nothing else happened. There was no wind that afternoon or that evening. Smoke went straight up until dark.

The sexton's name was Aalders and he had the keys and he went up the tower that evening and came down and said that the mechanism was as it always was, which is written into entry two in a different hand.

The wind came in from the north-north-west at about eleven the following morning and blew hard for two days.

That is entry one and it is written in a wheelwright's hand with the time to the nearest quarter hour and both bearings estimated by eye, and underneath it, in the same ink, is the sentence that made Femke sit down on the floor of her grandmother's spare room:

*Others have said this. Nobody writes it down.*

## Nineteen Hours

There are eleven hundred entries in that book.

Not eleven hundred turns. That is a distinction her great-aunt was firm about and that Femke has kept: an entry is an observation, and about a hundred and forty of them record the vane doing nothing on a day when somebody expected it to do something, which is exactly the sort of entry that a person keeping a record to prove a point would leave out.

Her great-grandfather kept it from 1951 until 1974, and then her grandfather until 2003, and then her great-aunt, who was the one who actually understood what she was doing, until 2019, and then nobody, and then Femke.

There is a gap. Between the November of 2019 and the March that Femke was twelve there are no entries, because her great-aunt died in the October and the book went into a drawer in a spare room with a quantity of other paper and nobody thought about it.

Femke found it looking for something else. Her grandmother said, when asked, "Oh, Truus's book," in the voice of somebody identifying a lampshade.

The pattern is not subtle and it does not need statistics to see, although Femke has done the statistics.

Her great-aunt did not do statistics and did not need to. She had read the book front to back perhaps thirty times and she could tell you, without looking, that the winter ones were good and the summer ones were rubbish and that anything under about eight hours tended to come to nothing.

All three of those held up when Femke put it in a spreadsheet, which she has always thought is the more interesting result.

The cockerel turns to a new bearing while the wind is still doing something else, and the wind arrives on that bearing somewhere between four and forty hours later, and the median is nineteen.

It does not do this every time. About a third of the entries are marked with a small cross, which was her great-aunt's notation for *nothing followed*, and Femke has worked out that the crosses cluster in summer.

It is much better in winter. It is best of all in the ten days either side of a deep low coming in off the Atlantic, which is the situation that matters in a town four metres below sea level behind a dyke.

Which is either the most significant thing in the book or the most obvious. Winter storms in the Netherlands come from a small number of directions in a small number of situations, and a vane that turned at random would look good in winter for no reason at all, and Femke has spent a good deal of time trying to work out how to separate those two and has not managed it.

The thing her great-aunt did, which nobody before her had done, was to start writing down the wind that actually arrived — bearing and force and time — in a second column.

Which is why the book is not a curiosity. It is a hundred and eleven pages of paired observations, and Femke had it digitised at nineteen, and it is now in a spreadsheet with eleven hundred rows, and she has done what you do with eleven hundred rows.

## What the Book Is For

The entry that matters is not from 1951.

It is from the thirty-first of January 1953, at eleven in the morning, in her great-grandfather's hand, and it reads: *N by NW, hard round, held. Told Bakker, told de Vries, told the sexton. Bakker moved his beasts.*

The North Sea flood came that night.

Femke is not going to make more of that entry than it will carry and she has thought about how to put this for a long time.

Nieuwe Sluis is not one of the places where the dyke went. The water came into that town to about knee height in the low streets and went down in three days and the losses were livestock and furniture. Nobody there was hurt. The places where it was catastrophic were in Zeeland and South Holland and there was nothing any weathervane could have done about any of it.

What the entry says is that on the morning of the thirty-first of January 1953, a wheelwright looked at a copper bird, and wrote down a bearing, and went and told three men, and one of them moved his cattle to the high field and the other two did not.

That is the whole of it. Two cows and a horse.

Femke has been to the church at Nieuwe Sluis on the thirty-first of January four times now, at eleven in the morning, and stood on the corner by the bakery and looked up.

She does not know what she is doing and she does not do it for a reason she can give. Bakker has been dead since 1988 and there is nobody in that town who knows the entry exists apart from her mother, who is not interested.

Femke is twenty-four and she works for the water board, which was not a coincidence.

She keeps the book. There are nineteen entries in her handwriting and the most recent is from the fourteenth of March and it has a cross beside it.

She has shown the spreadsheet to two colleagues who model surge, both of whom found it interesting and neither of whom could do anything with it, because the honest position is that eleven hundred bearings estimated by eye from a street over seventy years is not an instrument, and because the modern forecast is better than nineteen hours and is right far more often than two thirds of the time.

She writes in it anyway, at the kitchen table, in the same book, under her great-aunt's last entry.

And about four times a winter she goes out into the street at some point in the afternoon and stands on the corner by the bakery and looks up at a copper cockerel on a tower, in the way that nobody in that town has done since her great-aunt died — which is to say deliberately, for a minute at a time, on purpose, at a thing that is designed to turn.

She has seen it move twice.

The first was in the January she was nineteen and it was in a gust and it snapped round and hunted about and settled, which is what a vane does, and she wrote nothing.

The second was on the fourth of December 2024 at about twenty past two in the afternoon, in still air, and it went through perhaps eighty degrees in something over half a minute, smoothly, and stopped.

She stood on that corner for a further eleven minutes and then went home and got the book out and wrote the date and the time and two bearings under her great-aunt's hand.

The wind came in on the bearing she had written at about six the following morning, which is sixteen hours, which is inside the median.

That entry has no cross beside it. It also has no note, and no underline, and no exclamation mark, because there is a way that book is written and Femke is the fifth person to write in it.
