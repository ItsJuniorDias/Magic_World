## The Rule About the Fox

The rule was explained to Tam in the first week, at the bus stop, by a girl called Effie who had appointed herself to the job of explaining things and who was not always accurate.

The rule was that there was a fox in Fernaig wood, and that you only saw it when you were lost.

"Lost how?"

"Lost lost. Off the path, don't know where the road is, that kind." Effie was eating something out of a bag and did not appear to consider any of this remarkable. "My cousin saw it when he went in after a ball and came out the wrong side by the quarry. My mum saw it once. Everybody's seen it once."

"Have you?"

"No," said Effie, with some annoyance, "because I've lived here my whole life and I can't get lost in it, can I."

Tam had moved to Fernaig in the August from a town with a one-way system, and he had not been in the wood, and he filed the fox with the other information he had been given that week: that the shop shut at four on Wednesdays, that you did not sit in the third row on the bus, and that Mr Ross would let you off homework if you asked about the war.

Fernaig had a shop, a school with eighty-one pupils in it, a hall, and a wood that went up the back of everything and did not stop until the forestry track seventeen kilometres north. It had one bus in the morning and one at half three and a third on Fridays that people spoke about with some ceremony.

It had, as far as Tam could establish in his first fortnight, no way at all of arriving in it. Everybody there had always been there. He asked Effie who else had moved in recently and she thought about it seriously for some time and said the Polish family at the sawmill, in 2011.

He saw the fox for the first time on the twelfth of September, in the car park behind the school, at half past three in the afternoon.

It was sitting by the bins, in daylight, in the middle of a village, about five metres from the door he had just come out of. It was not doing anything. It was sitting the way a dog sits when it has been told to wait, front legs straight, looking at him.

Tam looked around the car park.

There were two teachers by the minibus and a group from the year above on the wall and Effie's brother locking up a bike, and not one of them had noticed a fox sitting by the bins in the middle of the afternoon.

He looked back and it was still there.

He walked to the bus with his heart going and did not tell anybody, because he had been in that school for four weeks and knew exactly how that conversation would go.

He looked it up that night, which is what you do. He found that foxes in villages are entirely normal and increasingly common and often bold in daylight, and that this is well documented, and that there was nothing whatsoever remarkable about a fox sitting by some bins.

He read four pages of that and felt better and went to sleep, and in the morning it was on the wall outside the shop.

## Everywhere

By the middle of October he had seen it thirty-one times and he had started counting because the counting made it feel like a project rather than a problem.

It was in the car park. It was on the wall outside the shop. It was on the road out to the caravan site at seven in the morning, and again at four, and once at both ends of the same day.

It was in his own garden.

That was on the eighteenth, and it was the one that stopped him sleeping. He got up for water at about one and looked out of the landing window because he always looked out of the landing window, and it was on the grass by the shed, sitting, facing the house.

It never came closer than about ten metres and it never went further than about thirty. It never ran. Twice he walked directly at it, in daylight, in a temper, and it stood up and moved off at a walk and stopped again when he stopped.

He tried, twice, to get somebody else to see it.

The first time was on the road by the caravan site with his mother in the car, and he said *there's a fox* in the most ordinary voice he could manage, and she said *oh yes* — and he spent the rest of the journey working out from what she said next that she had been agreeing with him rather than looking.

The second time was worse. It was on the school wall at lunch and he said it to Effie, and pointed, and she looked exactly where he was pointing for a good four seconds and said, "Where?"

He said he'd been mistaken. She was decent about it, which he minded.

He went into the wood in the end, in the last week of October, on a Saturday, on purpose, because he had worked out what was bothering him and could not make it stop bothering him any other way.

The rule was that you saw the fox when you were lost.

He was not lost. That was the point. He was not lost in Fernaig wood or anywhere else; he was in a car park, and a shop doorway, and his own garden, four metres from a back door he could have found blindfolded.

So either the rule was wrong, or Effie was wrong, or —

He went in at the top gate and walked for two hours, deliberately, badly, taking every wrong turn he could identify, and got himself thoroughly and genuinely lost somewhere in the middle by about half two.

He stood there in the wet bracken, properly disoriented, no idea which way the road was.

And there was no fox.

He came out an hour later at the quarry side, scratched and extremely cold, having established the one thing he had least wanted to establish, which was that the rule worked fine and that the problem was him.

He sat on the wall at the quarry for a while before going home, working it through.

If the rule was about woods, he was fine and the fox was a coincidence and foxes are common.

If the rule was not about woods, then something in Fernaig had decided he was lost — had been deciding it, daily, since the twelfth of September, in car parks and shop doorways and his own back garden — and had gone to the trouble of sitting down in his eyeline about thirty-one times to say so.

And the second one was ridiculous, and he believed it, and he could not think of a single person in that village he could say it to.

## What Lost Means

He told his mother in November and it went about as well as these things go, which is to say she was kind and completely missed it.

She said everybody found moving hard. She said it took a year. She said she had been miserable too when she was thirteen and Granny had moved them to Aberdeen, and that it had come good, and that he was doing so well and that Miss Cameron had said he was settling in beautifully.

All of which was true, and none of which was the thing.

He tried Mr Ross, obliquely, at the end of a Thursday, on the pretext of asking about local stories for a project he had not been set.

Mr Ross was about sixty and had been at that school for twenty-eight years and was not in a hurry.

He taught history to three year groups and ran the minibus and had a way of answering a question about forty seconds after you had given up on him.

He listened to the whole of Tam's very careful question about the fox — a question that mentioned folklore twice and did not mention Tam at all — and then he sat back and said, "How often are you seeing it?"

Tam looked at the desk.

"Most days," he said.

"Aye," said Mr Ross, and did not seem surprised, and did not say anything for a bit.

"The rule's about the wood," Tam said. "That's what everybody says. In the wood, off the path, don't know where the road is."

"That's what everybody says because that's where everybody's seen it, once, when they were eleven and daft." Mr Ross took his glasses off. "It's not a rule about woods, son. It's a rule about not knowing where you are."

Tam said nothing for a moment.

"That's the same thing."

"It is not remotely the same thing," said Mr Ross, "and you know fine it isn't, or you wouldn't have come in here with a project you've not been set."

"I know where I am."

"Do you."

Tam did not answer that.

"I've been here twenty-eight years," said Mr Ross, "and I came from Glasgow, and I saw that animal roughly every second day for the whole of my first winter, and I have not seen it since about 1999."

Tam sat there.

"Nobody else says that," he said. "Everybody says once. Everybody says they saw it once, off the path, when they were wee."

"Aye. Because everybody here got lost in a wood once when they were wee and found their way out by teatime." Mr Ross put his glasses back on. "You cannot be lost in a place you were born in. It is not available to you. Which is why the folk in this village think it's a story about a wood — because for them, it is."

He started stacking papers, which meant the conversation had about ninety seconds left.

"There's maybe four people in Fernaig it's not a story about a wood for. I'm one. You're one."

"Who are the other two?"

"That," said Mr Ross, "would be their business."

"What changed?"

"I stopped pretending I was fine," said Mr Ross, "which took me thirteen months and a very bad Christmas, and I would not particularly recommend the method."

## The Thing He Said Out Loud

He said it on the eighth of December, in the kitchen, at about nine at night, and he said it badly.

What he said was that he hated it.

He had not planned to. He had come down for a glass of milk. His mother had asked him a completely ordinary question about a form for a trip, and he had heard himself start talking and had been, for about the first thirty seconds, as surprised as she was.

He said he hated the bus and the shop shutting at four and that there was nothing, there was actually nothing, there was one shop. He said everybody had known each other since they were three and that they were not unkind about it and that was somehow worse. He said he had been at that school for four months and had not once been to anybody's house. He said that when they had left he had told everyone at his old school he'd be back at Christmas and that he had known when he said it that it was not true.

He said that everyone kept telling him he was settling in beautifully and that he had no idea who had told them that, because it had not been him.

His mother did not say it would come good.

She sat at the table and let him finish and then she said, "I know," and then, after a while, "I don't know what to do about most of that."

Which was, Tam thought later, the first accurate sentence anybody had said to him since August.

They talked for about two hours. Nothing was solved. The shop still shut at four.

He did not see the fox in December at all.

He noticed the absence about ten days in, on the way home on the bus, going past the wall by the turning where it had been every second Tuesday since October.

It was an odd feeling and not a good one at first. He had spent three months wanting it to stop and it had stopped, and the stopping did not feel like winning. It felt like the end of something he had been having a conversation with.

He looked. He is not going to pretend he did not look — he checked the garden from the landing window every night for three weeks like an idiot, and there was the shed and the grass and the wheelie bin and nothing else.

He saw it once more, in the following June, on a Tuesday, from the bus.

It was on the wall by the turning to the caravan site, sitting, front legs straight, and by then he was fourteen and had been to Effie's house fourteen times and was in the football, and he was not in the least lost, and he looked at it all the way past and it looked back.

He has thought about that one for years.

The version he has settled on is that it was not there for him at all. There were about thirty people on that bus.

But he is not certain, and the other possibility has never entirely gone away, which is that you do not stop being somebody who arrived from somewhere else, and that the fox knows this, and that once in a while it sits on a wall in the sunshine to remind you.

He is twenty-six now and he lives in Fernaig, which nobody who knew him at thirteen would have predicted, including him.
