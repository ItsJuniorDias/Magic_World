## How You Ask

Behind the houses at Ferrel there is a wood of holm oak and in it there are the mounds, and the mounds are the largest thing about that village that visitors never notice.

There are four of them and the biggest is chest high and about three metres across and it is made of needle litter and it has been there, according to a man who did a survey in 1998, for at least a century. It is a wood ant nest and there are perhaps a quarter of a million animals in it and it is entirely ordinary and there are ten thousand like it in Portugal.

Mariana has stood next to it a great many times. In August it is loud — you can hear it from about four metres, a dry continuous crackle that is nothing but a quarter of a million small feet on dry needles — and it is warm, properly warm, several degrees above the air, which is a thing wood ant nests do and which has been measured and is not mysterious.

There is nothing about that mound that would make anybody look at it twice. That is the point everybody in Ferrel makes when it comes up. It is a completely ordinary nest of a completely ordinary species that any child in southern Europe can find in an afternoon.

The way you ask is this.

You open the window on the wood side of the house, the whole window, at dusk, and you leave it open all night, and you do not shut it and you do not go into that room.

That is all. There is no phrase and no offering and nothing is left on the sill.

In the morning the house has stopped arguing.

That is the phrase. Not *the argument is over* and not *you have made up*. The house has stopped arguing, which is a claim about the house, and Mariana did not notice the grammar of it until she was about nineteen.

Mariana had known the mechanism since she was small and had never seen it done, because it is not done often. Her grandmother reckoned three or four times a year across a village of a hundred and sixty people. It is not a secret and it is not a ritual and there is no shame in it, and if you go past a house on the wood side at ten at night in April and the big window is open you look away, the way you would look away from a car with somebody sitting in it not driving.

Mariana saw an open window twice before she was twelve. The first was the Estrela house in 2016 and she was seven and did not know what she was looking at. The second was in 2019, in the summer, and she was ten and knew exactly what she was looking at and did not tell the person she was walking with, who was her friend Rita, whose house it was.

Rita has never mentioned it. Mariana has never mentioned it. They are still friends.

She asked her grandmother, at nine, what it cost.

Her grandmother said: "It costs the argument."

Mariana said that was the point.

And her grandmother said, "Yes," in the particular way she said yes when a conversation had reached the edge of what she was going to say, and Mariana did not get any further with it for four years.

She tried again at eleven with a better question, which was whether her grandmother had ever done it.

Her grandmother said that she had, once, in 1981, and that she was not going to say what about, and that Mariana was to leave it, and that if she ever needed to know more than that she would find out for herself and would not enjoy it.

## The Winter of the House

Her grandmother died in the February she was twelve and left the house at Ferrel to Mariana's mother and her mother's brother jointly, which is the most ordinary thing in Portugal and the beginning of a great deal of trouble.

The argument was about whether to sell it.

Her uncle was in Lisbon and had two children and a mortgage and wanted it sold, and he was not wrong. Her mother wanted to keep it and could not have told you exactly for what, and she was not wrong either. It was a stone house with a leaking roof in a village that has a shop three days a week, and it was worth something to a person from outside and nothing at all to anybody in Ferrel.

Mariana's parents argued about it from March to November.

Not constantly and not loudly. It is important to describe this accurately because it was not a shouting house. What it was, from March to November, was a house in which a specific subject was under everything — at dinner, in the car, when the phone went and it was Lisbon, in the silence after her mother came off the phone.

Mariana was twelve and she got very good that year at reading a room from the doorway.

The tells were: whether her mother's phone was face down on the table. Whether her father said *your brother* or *Zé*. Whether anybody mentioned the roof.

The roof was the worst one. The roof was leaking from about the June and everybody knew the roof was leaking, and it could not be discussed, because a roof is either worth fixing or it is not and that is the whole argument wearing a hat.

By September her mother had stopped sleeping properly and her father had started being extremely reasonable, which in that marriage was worse than anything.

By November the house had been empty for nine months with the roof going and nobody had been able to have a conversation that reached the end of itself.

Mariana opened the window on the fourteenth of November.

She did it at about seven in the evening, when her parents were out, in the room at the back that faces the oaks, and she opened it the whole way and put the catch on and went and sat in the kitchen with the door shut and did not go back in.

She had thought about it for nine days. She would like it on the record that she was not being impulsive and that she knew perfectly well that it was not her house and not her argument and not her decision, and that she did it anyway, at twelve, in November, and that if she could go back she is not certain what she would do.

## The Morning

Nothing happened in any sense that anybody could have filmed.

There were no ants in the house in the morning. Mariana went in at seven and the room was exactly as she had left it, cold, with the window open, and there was nothing on the sill and nothing on the floor and nothing on the wall, and she shut the window and went and had breakfast.

Her mother came down at half past.

She looked at the window, which Mariana had shut, and at Mariana, and said good morning, and put the kettle on. She had not slept properly since about August and she looked it and she was, that morning, ordinary.

And the thing that Mariana noticed, over the following four or five days, was not that the argument had stopped. It was that nobody was avoiding anything.

That was what had been in the house since March: a shape everybody was walking around. She had not known it was there until it was not, in the way you do not know a fridge is running.

The phone went from Lisbon on the Tuesday and her mother took it in the kitchen instead of going out to the yard, and talked about her uncle's daughter's school for eleven minutes, and put the phone down and made the tea and did not sit at the table afterwards with her hands flat on it.

Her father stopped being reasonable and went back to being ordinary, which was a considerable relief.

He argued with her about her phone on the Thursday, properly, at some volume, and Mariana went to bed almost cheerful.

Mariana did not tell anybody what she had done, and she did not feel guilty, and for about two years she thought she had got away with something clean.

She told her grandmother, in a manner of speaking, at the grave, in the February after, which she is aware is not the same as telling anybody.

## What Was Taken

The house at Ferrel is still empty.

The roof went properly in 2019 and there is a tarpaulin on it that Mariana's father put up and that has been there for six years. The gutter is off the back. There is a fig growing out of the wall by the chimney that was a seedling when she was fourteen and is now taller than she is.

Mariana goes and looks at it about twice a year. She has a key. She goes in and stands in the kitchen where her grandmother made her sit down and drink milk and told her, at nine, what the window cost, and then she comes out and locks it and drives back to Caldas.

The room at the back faces the oaks and the window in it does not open any more, because the frame has gone, and that is the only thing in that house that Mariana is glad about.

Nobody has sold it and nobody has fixed it, and the reason is not that they cannot agree.

The reason is that the question does not come up.

Mariana has watched this for eleven years and she can describe the mechanism exactly, because she has tested it. She has raised it herself, deliberately, at dinner, four times. It goes like this: she says the house, and her mother says yes, they must sort that out, and her uncle says the same thing on the phone at Christmas, and everybody agrees entirely, with real warmth, that something ought to be done.

And then it goes.

Not refused. Not deflected. It simply does not have any weight — the subject comes up and slides off and the conversation is on to something else, and if you ask anybody afterwards what was decided they will tell you, honestly, that it was a good talk and that they will get to it.

She has tried writing it down and putting it in front of them. She did that at twenty-one: two pages, the state of the roof, three options with numbers, a recommendation.

Her mother read it and said it was very good and that Mariana had always been the sensible one. Her uncle read it and rang up and was warm about it for twenty minutes. Nobody disagreed with a single line of it and nothing happened, and when she brought it up again at Christmas her mother could not remember which of the three options she had preferred.

Her mother and her uncle have not had a cross word in eleven years, which in a Portuguese inheritance is close to a miracle, and everybody in both families says so.

Mariana is twenty-three and she has said this out loud to one person, who was kind about it.

The person was a woman she shared a flat with in Coimbra, who listened to all of it and then asked one question, which was whether Mariana had ever told her mother.

She has not. She has thought about it perhaps four hundred times and she has worked out that there is no version of that conversation that does not end with her mother going and standing in front of the question for a while and then getting on with the dinner.

What she says is that the ants did exactly what they are asked to do and what her grandmother told her they do, and that nobody in Ferrel has ever misdescribed it. The argument was taken. It has not come back and it is not coming back.

What nobody had ever said to her — and she does not think anybody was hiding it, she thinks it is simply not the sort of thing that gets said to a child, and she is not sure she would have heard it at twelve — is that an argument is a thing standing on something.

They took the argument.

The house is what the argument was standing on, and the house is still there, with a fig in the wall, and there is nobody left in that family who can hold the question in their hands for long enough to answer it.
