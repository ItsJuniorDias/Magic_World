## Reading the Bar

The tidal range at Kerlouan is eight metres on a big spring and that number is the reason for everything about the place.

Eight metres means the harbour is a harbour for about five hours out of every twelve and a field of mud for the rest. It means the wall is enormous. It means every boat there has legs, and the whole of the working life of that village is arranged around a table of numbers that has been published in an almanac since 1839 and that is now on everybody's phone.

Maëlle grew up inside that arrangement the way other children grow up inside a school timetable. Meals moved. Her father's working day started at a different hour every day of her childhood and moved fifty minutes later each time, and she could tell you the state of the tide from the smell coming off the harbour before she could tell the time.

Everybody in Kerlouan does that arithmetic constantly and nobody there thinks of it as arithmetic.

The tables are extremely good. Maëlle would like that said first, because the story sounds otherwise like it is against them.

They are astronomy. The tide is the moon and the sun pulling on a rotating planet, and that is a solved problem, and the tables for Kerlouan are accurate to about four minutes and eleven centimetres a century in advance.

What the tables do not have is the weather.

A hard low pressure system lifts the whole sea. Wind piles it up against a coast or holds it off. On a bad day in Brittany the actual water can be sixty or seventy centimetres above what the table says, and once in a generation a great deal more than that, and the table cannot know because the table was calculated in 1974.

Which is what the shoal is for.

There is a bar across the mouth of that harbour and on the flood there is a shoal of mullet over it, and there has been for as long as the village has records of anything. Two or three hundred fish, grey, unremarkable, doing the entirely normal thing that mullet do in an estuary.

They are grey mullet and they are the least regarded fish on that coast. Nobody eats them if there is anything else. They come in on the flood to work the mud and they go out on the ebb and they have done that in every estuary in Europe since before there were estuaries in Europe, and there is nothing whatever unusual about the shoal at Kerlouan except that people used to look at it.

And where they sit tells you the water.

## The Men Who Could

Maëlle's grandfather could read them and so could about six other people in Kerlouan and by the time she was twelve there were two.

The reading is not mystical and he could describe it, and did, at length, to anybody who would sit still.

You look at the shoal about ninety minutes before high water, from the end of the wall, and you look at three things: how far up the bar they are sitting, whether they are strung out along the channel or bunched over the shallow, and how deep they are holding.

Bunched and shallow and well up the bar is more water coming than the table says.

He would say it exactly like that, in that order, every time, and then he would make her say it back.

Strung out down the channel and holding deep is less.

And there is a third state that his own father had a word for that does not translate — a nervousness, a shoal that will not settle in either position — and that means the water is going to arrive early and hard, and that you get everything above the line and you do it now.

Maëlle has seen the third state twice in eleven years. Her grandfather claimed to have seen it nine times in sixty. Le Guen, asked about it once at a funeral, held up four fingers and did not elaborate.

Maëlle asked him how it worked and he said he had no idea and that it was not his business to have an idea.

He was not being difficult. He genuinely did not think that knowing how a thing works and being able to use it were connected, and he had spent sixty-one years on that harbour among men who all thought the same, and Maëlle has since met a great many people with degrees who could learn something from that position and a great many others who could not.

She asked her physics teacher at fourteen and got a much better answer, which is that mullet in an estuary are exquisitely sensitive to salinity and to pressure and to the speed of the water over the bar, and that all three of those things start changing well before the water level does, and that a fish sitting in a current is a very good instrument for measuring that current.

The fish are not predicting anything. They are simply reacting, immediately and accurately, to conditions that a table calculated in 1974 has no access to.

Which is a better story than a mystery, and Maëlle has never got anybody in Kerlouan interested in it.

She tried, at fifteen, at a family lunch, with a diagram.

Her uncle said that was very interesting. Her father said that his father had been reading those fish before there was salinity, which is not an argument and which ended the conversation.

## The App

The app came in about 2016 and it is a good app.

It does the astronomy properly and it also pulls a surge model from the national service, which means it does have the weather in it — a forecast of it, on a grid, updated four times a day — and it is right about the water at Kerlouan very nearly all of the time.

The reading died in about four years.

Maëlle can date it, roughly, from who stopped coming to the wall. Four men stopped in 2017. Two more in 2019. Her grandfather went out every day until the September he was seventy-nine, and after that only when there was weather in it, which is what she does now.

Nobody decided to stop. There was no argument and nobody in that harbour ever said that the fish were nonsense. What happened is that a thing which took forty years to learn and ninety minutes of standing on a wall was replaced by a thing that took no time and required nothing, and it was right, and the men who could read the shoal got old.

That is the whole mechanism and Maëlle has stopped being angry about it, because when she is honest she cannot construct the argument for the other outcome.

What was anybody supposed to have done? Told a twenty-two-year-old with a mortgage to spend forty years learning a skill in order to be occasionally slightly better than a free app? The reading was not lost through carelessness. It was outcompeted, fairly, by something that works.

By the time Maëlle was twelve there were two: her grandfather, who was eighty-one, and a man called Le Guen who did not come down to the harbour any more.

She learned it because her grandfather was dying and because she was the only person in the family who would sit on that wall for ninety minutes, and she is entirely clear that she learned it for the wrong reason and got it by accident.

He did not set out to teach her. He set out to go and look at the fish, which he had done nearly every day of his life, and he could not get down the wall on his own by then and Maëlle was twelve and had nothing else on.

So she went with him. And he talked, because he had always talked, and about four months in she said something about the shoal being higher than yesterday and he stopped walking.

It took eleven months. She was not good at it. She is still not good at it — her grandfather could call the water to about fifteen centimetres and Maëlle, at twenty-four, can call it to about forty, which is the difference between forty years and eleven months.

He died in the October and she went out on the wall on the day of the funeral because she could not think what else to do with the afternoon, and the shoal was strung out down the channel and holding deep, and the water that evening was under the table by about thirty centimetres, and she got it right and there was nobody to tell.

## The Tuesday

The seventeenth of December was a Tuesday and the app said a high of six point one metres at eleven forty.

The surge model had it. There was a low coming through and the model had put about twenty centimetres on, and everybody in the harbour had looked at that on a phone on the Monday evening and had planned an ordinary day.

Maëlle went out on the wall at about ten because she went out on the wall.

The shoal was bunched, and shallow, and further up the bar than she had ever seen it.

She stood there for about eleven minutes doing the thing her grandfather had taught her, which is to look away and look back and see whether you get the same answer, and she got the same answer four times, and the answer was a great deal of water.

She had no number. She has never had a number; the reading does not give you a number, it gives you *more* or *less* or the third thing.

She rang her uncle, who was at the yard, and told him, and he said the app said six point one and she said she knew what the app said.

There was a pause on that phone that Maëlle has thought about a good deal since, because in it her uncle had to decide whether to move eleven boats on the say-so of a twelve-year-old and a fish.

He moved four of them. The four that were cheapest to move.

High water at Kerlouan on the seventeenth of December was six metres ninety-four at eleven fifty-one.

The low had deepened faster than the model had it and had come through further south, and the surge at Kerlouan was about eighty centimetres instead of twenty, and the national service issued a corrected bulletin at eleven fifteen which nobody in that harbour saw because nobody looks at a phone at eleven fifteen when the app told them at seven that it was an ordinary day.

Nothing terrible happened. Two boats took water over the transom and one man got extremely wet and there was a quantity of gear lost that everybody was cross about for a fortnight.

The four cheapest boats to move were fine.

Maëlle is twenty-four and she works for the harbour and she has the app on her phone and looks at it every single day.

Her job is mostly moorings and paperwork and telling visiting yachts about the sill.

She also goes out on the wall about ninety minutes before high water when there is weather in it, which is perhaps forty times a year, and she looks at the bar for eleven minutes.

She has never published anything and does not intend to. She has taught it to one person, who is nine, and who is her cousin's daughter, and who is bored by it.

What she says, when the men in the yard ask her about the Tuesday — and they do, in a way that is not entirely friendly and not entirely unfriendly — is that the app was not wrong.

The app had the best forecast anybody had. The fish had the water.
