## The Order of the Hill

Sleep came up the hill from the bottom, and it took about ninety minutes to get to the top, and everybody in Casal do Meio knew their own place in the order.

The bottom houses got it first. That was the tannery row, along the stream, six houses in a line with damp walls and the best sleep in the district — children down by half eight, adults gone by ten, nobody in that row awake past eleven for as long as anybody could remember.

Then the middle: the square, the shop, the six or seven houses behind the church. Then the terrace on the bend. Then the four houses at the top, where Duarte lived, which got it last, and which had got it last since before his grandmother was born.

The top houses were a joke in that village and had been for generations. Anybody at the top who complained about being tired was told they had brought it on themselves by living up there, which made no sense and was said with great satisfaction.

There was a rhyme about it that Duarte's grandmother knew four lines of and that nobody knew the rest of, and there was a thing the tannery row said about the top houses at weddings, and there was a particular way of saying *ah, you're from the top* that had been in use in that village since at least the 1930s.

None of it was unkind. All of it was extremely tiresome if you happened to be twelve and living in one of the four.

Duarte had never much minded until he was twelve, when he started at the school in the town and had to be at the bus at ten past seven.

Ninety minutes is nothing when you are nine and there is no bus. Ninety minutes is a considerable amount of your life when you are getting up at a quarter past six five days a week, and Duarte spent the whole of that autumn doing an arithmetic that had never occurred to anybody in his family: that over a school year the top of the hill loses something in the region of two hundred and seventy hours to the bottom of it.

He put this to his mother, who said that was very interesting and asked whether he had done his Portuguese.

The donkey came up at about half past eight.

You did not have to watch for her. Everybody in Casal do Meio has seen her at some point, usually by accident, usually from a window, and the thing they all say is the same: she is entirely ordinary. She is a small grey donkey with a dark cross on her shoulders and one ear that goes sideways, and she comes up the road at a walk, and she is carrying two panniers, and the panniers are full.

Full of what is not a question anybody in that village asks, and Duarte had learned by about six that asking got you a look.

He had got the look from four different adults by the time he was eight and had eventually stopped, and the interesting thing — the thing he only understood as a grown man — is that the look was not disapproval. It was embarrassment. Nobody knew, and nobody had ever known, and a village does not enjoy being asked a question about a thing it has lived beside for two hundred years without ever once looking into.

She goes up the road and turns in at the tannery row first and stands in the middle of it for a bit. Then the square. Then the terrace. Then, last, up the track to the four houses at the top, where she arrives at about ten o'clock, and after that the whole village is asleep and the road is empty.

## The Week She Was Lame

She came up on the Monday and there was something wrong with her off fore.

Duarte saw it from the window because he was awake, because he was always awake, and because at the top of the hill you have a long time to fill between eight and ten. She came round the bend and she was not walking wrong exactly — she was walking short. A shortened step on the right, and a pause at the top of it, of the kind you would notice in an animal you had watched for years and in no other circumstance.

She did the tannery row and the square and the terrace at about the usual time.

She got to the top at twenty to one in the morning.

Duarte went to school on the Tuesday having had about four hours and spent the whole of the bus journey with his forehead against the window, and got seven out of twenty in a maths test he had actually revised for.

Duarte knows the time because he had been lying in the dark for four hours by then in a state he had never been in before, which was not being unable to sleep so much as sleep simply not having arrived in the room. There is a difference and anybody who has had a bad week knows it.

Tuesday she got there at ten past one.

Wednesday she did not get there at all.

That was the worst of it and it was not the tiredness. He had been tired before. What Wednesday night had in it was the other thing — the specific experience of lying in a bed, in the correct position, with the light off, at the correct hour, and finding that the room simply did not contain the material.

It is not the same as insomnia and Duarte has been careful about that distinction his whole life, because he has since had ordinary insomnia and it is nothing like it. Insomnia is a mind that will not stop. This was a mind perfectly willing to stop and nothing there to stop into.

He heard her turn back at the terrace — the hooves stopping, the long pause, and then the hooves going away down again — and he lay in his bed at the top of Casal do Meio and stared at a ceiling he could not see until the light came up behind the ridge at about six.

On Thursday morning there were four households at the top of that hill in a state that the rest of the village found extremely funny for about a day and then stopped finding funny.

The stopping was quite sudden and it happened at the shop.

Senhora Aurora came down on the Thursday for bread, which she did every day, and she stood at the counter and could not remember what she had come for, and stood there working at it in front of five people for the better part of a minute.

She was eighty-one and there was nothing whatsoever wrong with her memory. Everybody in that shop knew it. And by that afternoon the joke about the top of the hill had gone out of circulation and did not come back for years.

## What They Tried

Senhora Aurora at the top house, who was eighty-one and had lived up there her whole life, called a meeting in her kitchen, which meant four adults, Duarte, and Duarte's little sister who was asleep on a chair by nine, which nobody remarked on because she was six and six-year-olds get theirs early wherever they live.

They went through the options.

It should be said that this was, by the standards of Casal do Meio, an extraordinary event. There had never been a meeting about the donkey. In two hundred years of that hill, as far as any of them could establish, nobody had ever sat down in a kitchen and discussed her out loud with other adults present, and all four of them were visibly uncomfortable about doing it.

The first was to move down. This is not as ridiculous as it sounds — there were two empty houses in the middle that week — and it was rejected in under a minute by everybody, and Duarte noticed that nobody gave a reason.

The second was to go down and meet her. Senhora Aurora's son-in-law had tried this on the Tuesday: had walked down at half nine and stood at the terrace, in the road, and waited for her to come past.

He said that she had come past. He said that she had gone round him. He said, with some difficulty, that he had walked back up feeling exactly as awake as when he set out, and that he was not going to try it again and would rather not discuss it.

Duarte asked him one question about it, weeks later, when the whole business was over, and got one answer.

He asked what it had been like when she went round him.

The son-in-law thought about it and said: "Like being furniture."

The third was to do something about the foot.

That was Duarte's, and it got the reaction that ideas from twelve-year-olds get in a room of adults, which is a pause and then somebody else saying it again slightly differently eleven minutes later.

The trouble with the third option was that nobody in Casal do Meio had ever touched her. There was no tradition of it. She was not anybody's donkey and had never been in anybody's field and did not, as far as anyone knew, exist during the day.

Senhora Aurora said that her mother had told her that you leave her alone and she comes, and that in eighty-one years she had never heard of anybody trying anything else, and that there was probably a reason for that.

Duarte asked what the reason was.

Senhora Aurora said she had no idea and that this was exactly the sort of question that had made his grandfather difficult.

She said it warmly, and Duarte took it as a compliment, and it was probably meant as one.

The meeting broke up at about eleven with nothing decided and four adults going home to houses in which they were about to lie awake, and Duarte's mother put a hand on the back of his neck on the way out of the door and did not say anything, which in that family was a whole conversation.

## The Farrier's Rasp

He did it on the Friday, on his own, without telling anybody, which he now accepts was not a good decision and which he would do again.

He went down at half eight with his grandfather's farrier's rasp — the old one, in the shed, from when there had been mules on that hill — and a torch he did not turn on, and he sat on the wall at the bottom of the terrace and waited.

She came at twenty past nine, going short on the off fore.

He did not stand in the road. That was the whole of his plan and it was the only part he had thought about properly: he had watched Senhora Aurora's son-in-law stand in the road and get gone round, and he had decided that the mistake had been the standing.

So he stayed on the wall and let her come past, and then he said, to her back, in a completely normal voice:

"It's your right front and I've got a rasp."

She stopped.

Duarte has told this part perhaps four times in his life and he has never told it well, and what he says is that she stopped, and stood for a moment, and then turned round in the road and walked back to the wall and put her nose against his knee.

It took a long time. He was twelve and he had used a rasp exactly twice, on a horse belonging to a neighbour, under supervision, three years earlier. What was in the foot was a stone — not a dramatic one, a flat grey stone the size of a thumbnail, wedged between the wall and the frog, which is exactly where they wedge, and which had been there since about the previous Sunday.

He got it out and rasped the wall down a little where it had grown over, because his grandfather had told him once that you always tidy the edge, and he was not certain that was right and did it anyway.

Then she put the foot down and stood on it, and walked four paces up the road and back, and stood in front of him again.

Duarte says the next bit is the part he cannot explain and would not want to.

She was carrying two panniers. He had never been closer to them than fifty metres. From a metre away in the dark he could see that they were ordinary panniers of woven cane, the kind still made in Barcelos, and that they were full, and that what was in them was not anything.

She went up the hill at twenty past ten and the top houses were asleep by eleven.

Duarte is forty. He lives in the middle now, behind the church, which he did not plan and which his mother has never let him forget.

He goes back up to the top house on Sundays because Senhora Aurora is ninety-nine and has outlived everybody who ever laughed at the order of that hill.

And once every year or two he wakes at about half eight for no reason and lies there and hears, from the road below, the sound of an animal going up at a walk, on four sound feet, entirely ordinary, on its way to somebody else.
