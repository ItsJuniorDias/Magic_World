## The Late Spring

Spring was four weeks late and everyone in the valley had a theory.

Mr Varga at the garage said it was the jet stream, and said it in the tone of a man who had recently learned what a jet stream was. The women who came up from Brod on the Thursday bus said it was because the winter had been mild and the year was making up for it. Father Emil said it was not for us to know, which Tolek noticed was the answer Father Emil gave to almost everything, and which had the advantage of always being technically correct.

Tolek's mother said the weather owed nobody an explanation, and told him to bring the washing in.

But the almond tree by the church had put out buds in February and then thought better of it.

That was the part nobody had a theory for. The buds had come, small and pink at the tips, in a warm week at the end of winter. And then they had stopped. They had not opened and they had not fallen. They had simply shut, and gone hard, and stayed like that on the branches for nine weeks — hundreds of little closed fists, waiting.

Old trees do not do that. Old trees commit.

And the snow on the high field was not melting.

It was not falling either. That was the other thing. There had been no new snow since the second week of March, and there had been sun, real sun, enough to warm the stones on the south wall. But the white line across the top of the mountain had not moved down by so much as a metre. It sat where it was, exactly where it was, with an edge as clean as a page.

Tolek walked up to it after school on a Tuesday, with his hands pulled inside his sleeves.

He was not being brave. He wanted to know, and the wanting had got bigger than the not-wanting over about fourteen days, which is how most brave things actually happen.

At the edge of the snow he stopped and looked at the ground.

There were tracks going up.

They were enormous — deep, round, with the claw marks well out in front of the pad — and there were no tracks coming down. Not old ones filled in with new snow, because there had been no new snow. None at all.

He stood there for a while doing the arithmetic that everybody does at that moment, which is: *if it went up, and it has not come down, then it is up there.*

Then he followed the tracks, because he was twelve, and because some questions will not wait until you are older.

## The Bear Who Would Not Wake

The tracks went up through the thin birch and across the shoulder of the hill and stopped at a hollow under a leaning rock, and the bear was in it.

She was asleep.

Tolek's first thought, which he was not proud of afterwards, was that she was much too big. Everything he had been told about bears had been told to him by people describing bears, and describing is not the same as seeing. She filled the hollow the way water fills a cup.

His second thought was that he was not frightened, and that this was strange, and that he should probably examine it later when he was somewhere else.

The cold was coming off her.

He could see it. That is the part he could never explain properly, not to his mother and not to anyone since. You know how heat comes off a road in August — that shiver in the air, that wobble? It was that, but backwards, and going down instead of up. It poured off her shoulders and pooled in the hollow and ran out over the lip of it, and where it ran, frost stood up on the grass in little white fences.

She was breathing. Very slowly. He counted, and it was nine seconds in and thirteen out, which is slower than seemed reasonable for something that size.

There was snow in her fur. Not on it — in it, down between the hairs, right against the skin, and it was not melting even there. When he finally came close enough to look properly he could see individual flakes, whole and six-sided and perfect, months old, held there as if the calendar had not been told.

He sat down at what he judged to be a polite distance, which was about four metres, on a stone that turned out to be very cold.

And sitting there, in the quiet, with the frost fences growing at his feet, he understood the thing that had brought him up the mountain, and it arrived complete, without anybody telling him.

She was not causing the winter.

She was holding it.

Somebody had to carry the cold from one year to the next. It could not simply stop; it had to be taken somewhere and kept until it was needed again. And the carrying was heavy — you only had to look at her to see how heavy — and she had been carrying it since October and she had not been able to put it down.

He did not know why she could not put it down. He thought about it a great deal and never worked it out. Some things are heavy for reasons that are nobody's business.

He went down before dark. The tracks he made going down were the only tracks going down.

## What the Valley Said

He made the mistake of telling his brother.

Marek was fifteen and had recently become impossible in the specific way that fifteen-year-olds become impossible, which is that he had stopped being interested in anything and had started being interested in not being interested in things.

Tolek told him because he had to tell somebody. He had been up the mountain three times by then and the knowledge had got too big to carry alone; it kept coming up in his throat at the dinner table.

Marek listened to the whole thing.

Then he said: "It's a bear."

"I know it's a bear."

"Bears sleep. That's what they do. It's the entire thing bears are known for." He was not being cruel. That was somehow worse. He was being reasonable, patiently, the way you are reasonable with somebody who is quite young. "It'll wake up when it's hungry and go and eat something and you won't want to be sitting four metres away when it does."

"The snow isn't melting round her."

"The snow isn't melting because it's a north face and it's in shadow until half past two."

Tolek had no answer to that, because it was true. It was a north face and it was in shadow until half past two, and that fact had been sitting there the whole time being an excellent explanation for something that was not what was happening.

That is the trouble with a good reason. It does not have to be the right one to be difficult to argue with.

Marek came up with him on the Saturday. He came up in the way older brothers come, complaining about the walk and going too fast and stopping to wait with his hands on his hips.

At the edge of the snow he stopped complaining.

Tolek watched him look at the line — that clean straight edge running across the mountain like the top of a page — and then look down at the frost fences standing up in the grass, in April, at two in the afternoon, in the sun.

They went up to the hollow together and Marek looked at the bear for about ninety seconds and then turned round and went back down the mountain without saying anything at all.

He did not speak to Tolek for two days.

On the third day he came into the room where Tolek was doing his homework and stood in the doorway and said: "Don't tell Mum."

"I wasn't going to."

"Don't tell anybody." He was holding the door frame with one hand. "You know what happens if you tell people. Somebody rings the forestry office and they come up with a truck, and they'll be right to, because it's a bear and there's a school in the valley. And they'll do it properly and humanely and everything and it will still be a truck coming up the mountain."

Tolek had not thought that far.

He thought about it for a long time afterwards — that his brother, who was impossible, who had not been interested in anything for a year and a half, had gone up the mountain, looked at a sleeping animal for ninety seconds, and immediately understood the whole shape of the danger while Tolek was still working out how to describe the frost.

"Are you coming up again?" Marek asked.

"Yes."

His brother nodded slowly, and looked at the floor, and said, "Take the red coat, not that one. That one's too thin and Mum'll notice you shivering."

And he went out and shut the door.

They never discussed it again. Not that spring, not that summer, not in the thirteen years afterwards while their mother was alive, and not at the funeral, and not since.

But Marek asked him one thing, at the funeral, standing at the back with a paper cup of coffee, out of nowhere, after a silence of about four minutes.

"That thing on the mountain," he said. "Did it ever wake up?"

And Tolek said: "No. Not while I was there."

And Marek said, "Right," and they went back in.

## Letting Go of the Cold

He came back the next day. And the day after that.

He did not do anything clever.

That is the part he told his own children, years later, when they asked, and they were always disappointed by it. He did not sing to her. He did not bring her anything, because what do you bring. He did not try to wake her, because it seemed to him that waking somebody who is carrying something heavy is a good way to make them drop it on their foot.

He sat on the cold stone at four metres, and he stayed for an hour, and then he went home.

Sometimes he read out loud, because the silence up there had a quality that made him want to put something into it. He read a book about a shipwreck, which he had read twice already, and he skipped the boring middle both times.

Once he told her, at length and with considerable feeling, about a disagreement he had had with his brother — a disagreement which had seemed enormous at eight in the morning and which had somehow got smaller and smaller as he described it, until by the end of the telling it had shrunk to roughly the size of a misunderstanding about a bicycle pump, which is what it had actually been all along.

He talked to her for fourteen days.

On the eleventh day the snow around the hollow had gone soft at the edges. He noticed it on the way up: the clean line had lost its cleanness. It had begun to fray.

On the twelfth day there was water running under the snow. He could hear it before he could see it — a thin sound, very cold, like a wire being drawn — and when he knelt down and pushed the crust with his thumb it came away and there were threads of water underneath, going down the mountain in a hurry, as though they had been waiting a long time and had somewhere to be.

The bear did not wake while he was there. Not once, in twelve days.

He went up on the thirteenth day and the hollow was empty.

It was warm. That was the thing that stopped him at the edge of it — the hollow was warm, and it smelled of wet stone and crushed grass and, faintly, of animal, and there was a shallow dish pressed into the ground where something very large had lain for a very long time.

There were no tracks leading away.

He looked. He looked for most of an hour, in widening circles, and the ground was soft enough that there should have been tracks, and there were none.

He went down the mountain with his coat over his arm because he did not need it.

In the valley the almond tree by the church had opened.

All of it. Every bud on every branch, all at once, in a single afternoon, as though the tree had been standing there for nine weeks with its arms folded waiting for somebody to give it permission. The petals were coming off it already in the warm air and going along the road in drifts, and two of the Sokol children were running through them, and the smell of it reached him before he got to the gate.

His mother was in the doorway with a basket on her hip.

"There you are," she said. "Spring, at last. About time."

"Did you see the tree?"

"I saw it. Sixty years I've been looking at that tree." She shifted the basket. "The weather owes nobody an explanation."

Tolek considered telling her.

He thought about it the whole way across the yard, and he decided — for reasons he could not have given you at the time and could give you perfectly well now — that it was not his to tell.

"No," he agreed. "It doesn't."

And he went to wash his hands.
