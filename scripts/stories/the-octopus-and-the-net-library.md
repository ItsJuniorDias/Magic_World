## The Wrong Kind of Rubbish

Everything a net brings up that is not a fish gets thrown back, and Iro had been throwing it back since she was seven.

That was the job. It was her job on her uncle's boat and it had been her mother's job on the same boat and it was, as far as Iro could tell, the entry-level position in the whole of the eastern Aegean. You stand at the sorting table with the catch coming out of the net and you pull out what does not belong, and the fish go one way and the rest goes over the side.

The rest is mostly weed and stones and plastic.

Mostly plastic, if she is honest, and the proportion had changed in her lifetime in a way that nobody on that boat ever said out loud. Her uncle had a word for the whole category and the word was not complimentary and it covered a fifteenth-century amphora sherd and a supermarket bag equally.

But not entirely. Over six years Iro had put back a bicycle wheel, a printer, a great many single shoes, a mannequin's arm that had caused a considerable amount of shouting, and, on one morning in the March she was ten, a brass compass with a cracked glass that had made her uncle stop what he was doing and hold it for a while and then say, "Over."

She had said it seemed a shame.

"It's a compass with no glass off a boat that sank before I was born," he said. "What do you want me to do with it? Put it in the house?"

There was a version of the argument Iro could have made and did not, at ten, because she did not have the words for it yet. It was that the compass was not rubbish and was not treasure and that the boat had no category for a third thing.

She had thought about that for years afterwards, because he was right and because she had also been right, and she had never worked out how both could be true at once.

The compass went over the side at about half past five in the morning three miles off Panormos, and Iro watched it go down through water so clear that she could follow it for what felt like an extremely long way.

And something came up and took it.

Not a fish. Fish do not do that. What came up out of the dark under the boat was pale and enormous and moved in a way that made her stomach turn over, and it came up perhaps four metres, and one arm went out and closed on the compass in mid-water, and the whole of it folded and was gone.

Iro did not say anything to her uncle. She has never been able to explain why, and the closest she can get is that she was ten, and she had watched something happen that was clearly none of her business.

She thought about it for the rest of that day and most of that year.

The obvious explanation was the right one and she knew it even then: an octopus took a shiny object in mid-water, which is a thing they do, and which is on film, and which nobody who works on that sea would find worth mentioning at breakfast.

What stopped her was the timing. It had come up before the compass reached it. She had watched the brass going down and she had watched the pale thing coming up to meet it, and the two had arrived at the same point at the same moment, which meant it had started before it could have seen the compass at all.

It had started when the compass hit the water.

## Eight Shelves

She found the shelves the following August, free-diving off the rocks at Agriolivado, which she was not supposed to do alone and did every day of every summer holiday of her childhood.

There is a ledge down there at about eight metres where the rock steps out and then goes on down into a slot too narrow for a person. Iro had known that ledge since she was small. What she had not known — because you cannot see it from above and because you have to be lying flat and looking sideways — was that under the overhang the rock has eight horizontal cracks in it, running back into the dark, one above the other, like a bookcase left out in the weather.

They were full.

She got about a second and a half of looking on the first dive, and eleven or twelve seconds on the fourth, and by the end of that August she had a fair idea.

Eight metres is not deep. Eight metres is a nuisance: it is deep enough that you have perhaps twenty usable seconds on the bottom at thirteen years old, and shallow enough that you feel foolish for not having more.

She went down that ledge perhaps two hundred times that August. She did it in the flat hour after lunch when the water goes glassy and the boats are in, and she did it with a mask that leaked, and she came up each time with about two shelves' worth of information in her head and lost half of it on the way to the surface.

A shelf of cups. Not one kind — a tin mug, a china cup with no handle, a plastic beaker, a thing that might have been a chalice and was probably a trophy.

A shelf of things you hold up to your eye. The compass was on it. So were a pair of spectacles, a small brass telescope in bad condition, and something Iro is fairly sure was a broken camera.

A shelf of shoes, all of them singles.

A shelf of things with edges: knives, a plane blade, half a pair of scissors, a razor.

And so on, eight of them, down to the bottom shelf, which was the hardest to see and which held things Iro could never quite categorise until she was about fifteen and worked it out in the middle of a maths lesson: they were all things that had held something else. A tin. A wallet. A box. A shoe would have gone on the shoe shelf, but a purse went here.

She has never seen the octopus at the shelves. She wants that stated plainly, because it is the weakest part of the whole account and because people always assume she has.

She has seen one on that ledge twice in thirty years, both times moving, neither time doing anything. The den — if there is a den — is somewhere down the slot, and the slot goes into the rock at an angle and Iro has looked into it with a torch and seen nothing but rock.

The arrangement is not by material and it is not by size and it is not by age. There is a plastic beaker two centimetres from a bronze cup.

It is by what the thing was for.

## The Argument With Her Uncle

She told him when she was fifteen, and she chose the moment badly, which was on the boat, at the sorting table, with a net coming up.

He listened to the whole thing without stopping work, which is his way, and at the end of it he said: "So there's an octopus with a shelf of cups."

"Eight shelves."

"An octopus," said her uncle, "with eight shelves." He put a fish in the box. "Iro, they collect. That is a thing they do. They pick up shells and stones and bottle tops and they pile them round the den. There's a word for it. It's in the books."

She said she knew that. She said she had read the books, which was true; she had read every book about them the library on the island had, which was two.

She said that piling things round a den is not the same as putting all the cups on one shelf and all the shoes on another.

Her uncle worked for a while without answering, which with him is not avoidance; it is the answer being assembled.

"So it likes cups."

He said it without any cruelty at all, which made it worse, and he handed her a bucket.

"It doesn't like cups. There's a plastic beaker next to a bronze one. It's not liking anything, it's *sorting* —"

"For what?"

And Iro stopped, because she had no answer, and because the thing her uncle had done was the only useful thing anybody did for her in six years of thinking about it.

*For what.*

An octopus that piles shells round a den is doing something with an obvious purpose: it is building a wall. Every book says so. The pile is a defence and the animal moves in behind it.

Nothing on those eight shelves was a wall.

She measured it, eventually, at nineteen, with a slate and a pencil, over four dives. The shelves are set back under an overhang in cracks too shallow to hide in, arranged along a face that faces out. If you wanted to hide behind that stuff you would have to bring it out and heap it up, which would take about an hour, and would work, and is not what has been done.

The things are set out.

There is a difference between a heap and a display and every human being alive can tell them apart at a glance, and Iro has never met anybody who could say what the difference consists of.

They are set out where a thing swimming past at the level of the ledge would see them, and they are set out in an order, and the order is not one that any octopus has any use for whatsoever — because *what a thing is for* is a fact about people. A cup is only a cup if you know about drinking. A compass is a lump of brass unless you know about being lost.

## What It Is For

Iro is thirty-nine. She works for the port authority and she dives most weekends and the ledge at Agriolivado is still there.

The shelves are still there. Some of the things have changed. The mannequin's arm turned up on the fourth shelf about eleven years ago, which she took personally and which she has never mentioned to her uncle, who is seventy-six and who has not been over the side of a boat since 2009 and who still says *over* about anything that is not a fish.

She has had a long time to think about it and she has three positions, and she holds all three at once, which she has stopped apologising for.

The first is her uncle's and it is almost certainly correct. Octopuses collect. They are extremely clever and they manipulate objects constantly and they arrange the things round a den. Any pattern she has found in eight cracks in a rock is a pattern she has brought with her, and a person who dives the same ledge for thirty years will find whatever they went down for. This position is dull and well supported and she has never been able to knock it over.

The second is that the order is real. She has photographs. She has, in a folder, six hundred pictures of those eight shelves taken over twenty years, and the categories hold. Things have moved shelf — the broken camera was on the edges shelf for a season and then was not — and every move she has recorded has been in the direction of a better fit, never a worse one.

The third is the one she does not say out loud very often.

She said it once, at a conference in Heraklion, to a marine biologist she had been talking to for about an hour and who had been extremely interested up to that point. The conversation ended shortly afterwards in a friendly way and the woman was perfectly nice about it.

Iro does not blame her and would probably have done the same.

It is that the shelves are not for the octopus.

Everything about them is arranged for something with eyes at the height of that ledge, that swims past, that understands what a cup is. There is exactly one animal in that sea that fits, and it is the animal that has been throwing things over the side of boats off Panormos since before anybody kept records.

Somebody down there has been sorting our rubbish for a very long time, by what we made it for, and putting it out along a face where we would find it.

Iro has never taken anything off those shelves.

She took the compass back once, in 2011, having found a second one in a junk shop in Kalymnos — the same pattern, the glass intact — and she put it on the ledge at the end of the second shelf, and came up, and went home.

It was on the shelf, in the row, in the right place, the following weekend.

It has been there fourteen years and it has never moved, and she checks every time, and she has never worked out whether that means it was accepted or whether it means nothing at all.
