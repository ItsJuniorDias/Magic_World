## The Third Bollard

The gull put things on the third bollard from the steps, and only on the third.

Nobody knew why the third. It was not the biggest or the nearest or the one that got the sun. It was a bollard exactly like the four on either side of it, cast iron, painted white about nine times, with a chip out of the top where somebody's rope had been.

Things appeared on it. Not often — four or five times a year, no more — and always overnight, and always after weather.

A green plastic torch. A single earring, gold, bent. A tobacco tin with somebody's initials scratched in it that turned out to be nobody's initials anybody recognised. A watch that did not go. A folding knife with the blade rusted open. A hair slide with two of the four stones missing.

And, since before Nél was born, the small blue shoe.

There was a book, as well, though nobody called it a book. It was a school exercise book in the harbourmaster's office with a list in four or five different handwritings: the date, what had appeared, and whether it had gone.

It started in 1974, which was when somebody had finally thought to start it, and it had five pages used.

Nél had read the whole thing in an afternoon when she was ten, standing up, because she had not been exactly given permission.

*14.xi.81 — spectacles, one arm missing. Claimed 19.xi by Sr. Bento.*
*3.iii.86 — small anchor, brass, ornamental. Not claimed.*
*22.ix.94 — mobile telephone. Not claimed (obviously).*

The *(obviously)* had been added in different ink, later, by somebody with a sense of humour.

The shoe was the only one that had ever stayed.

Everything else got claimed, usually within a week, usually by somebody who came down and stood in front of the bollard for a while first.

The standing in front of it was not a formality and nobody had ever decided it should happen. It simply did. A thing appeared, word went round the harbour by lunchtime, and over the following days people came down and looked at it, one at a time, and most of them went away again.

Nél watched four claimings before she was twelve and they were all the same shape.

The person would come down the steps and along the wall and stop, and they would not touch it at first. There would be a pause of about a minute in which they did not touch it. Then they would pick it up and turn it over once and put it in a pocket and walk back up the steps, and that would be the whole of it.

Nobody ever announced anything. Nobody ever said *that's mine*. Somebody would ask Dona Lurdes the next day and she would say *the earring's gone*, and that was how the harbour found out. The tobacco tin went to a man from Cabo who did not explain himself. The torch went back to a boy in Nél's own class who had dropped it off the ferry and had been shouted at about it.

The shoe had been on that bollard, more or less, for seventy years.

There were three photographs of it that Nél knew about. One from a newspaper in 1979, in an article about the town that got almost everything else wrong. One taken by a Dutch couple in about 2004 and put on the internet, where it had attracted thirteen comments, all of them from people inventing worse versions of the story. And one on the wall of the café, black and white, from some time in the sixties, in which the shoe is on the bollard and there is a boy in the background who is now a man who runs the chandlery.

It was not always on the bollard. People had moved it. It had been in the harbourmaster's office, in a drawer, for a while in the nineteen-eighties. It had been on a shelf in the café. It had been, for one summer, in a small glass box which everybody agreed afterwards had been a mistake.

Every time, within a year or two, somebody had quietly put it back out on the third bollard, because that was where it went.

## What the Rule Is

Nél was eleven and had recently become the sort of person who wanted the rule stated properly.

She got a lot of different answers.

The clearest came from Dona Lurdes at the café, who had been in that harbour since 1961 and who set it out for her over a cup of milky coffee that Nél had not asked for and drank anyway.

The rule, said Dona Lurdes, was this. The gull does not bring you things. The gull brings back things. There is an enormous difference and half the town gets it wrong.

Everything on that bollard, ever, has been something the sea took off somebody. Not something lost inland and washed down the river. Not something dropped on the beach. Something the water took: off a boat, off the wall, out of a pocket in a swell.

"And she brings it back?"

"She puts it where a person can find it. That's all I'll say she does. What you make of that is your affair."

Nél asked how long the gull had been doing it, expecting an answer in years.

Dona Lurdes said that was the wrong question and that Nél was clever enough to work out why, which took Nél about four seconds and then made her sit back in her chair.

Gulls live for twenty or thirty years. Some go longer. Not seventy.

"So it's not the same gull."

"No."

"Then who taught it?"

Dona Lurdes drank her coffee.

"That," she said, "is the question I have been not answering for sixty years, and I am not going to spoil my record for you."

## The Shoe

Nél took the shoe seriously in the way that only somebody eleven can take a thing seriously.

She measured it. It was fourteen centimetres, which she looked up, which made it roughly a size five, which made it a child of about eighteen months to two years.

It was leather, and it had been blue, and it was now the colour of a blue thing that has spent a long time somewhere. It had a strap and a small button and the button was still on. Somebody, at some point, had put a stitch into the strap by hand with a thread that did not match, which Nél found harder to look at than anything else about it.

She thought about that stitch a great deal.

It was not a repair the shoe needed. She looked at it closely, several times, and the strap was not torn and had never been torn. Somebody had put a stitch into a strap that was perfectly fine, in the wrong colour, by hand.

Which meant somebody had done it after. On the wall, or in a kitchen, or in the harbourmaster's drawer — somebody at some point in seventy years had picked this thing up and sat down with a needle and put one stitch into it for no practical reason whatsoever.

Nél asked four adults and none of them knew, and two of them looked at her differently for asking.

She was, at first, entirely certain of what it meant. She was eleven and she had read books.

She took this theory to her father, who put down what he was doing, which he did not usually do.

"No," he said. "No. Nobody drowned. Not a child, not ever, not in this harbour. I want you to hear that properly."

"How do you know?"

"Because it's a harbour town and that is precisely the kind of thing a harbour town knows to the exact date." He picked his work back up. "There's a list in the church with every soul this water has taken since 1804 and it is not a long list and there is not a child on it. You can go and read it. I did, when I was about your age, for the same reason."

Nél went and read it. It took her forty minutes and she was quiet for two days afterwards, but not the kind of quiet she had been expecting.

The list was cut into two slate panels on the north wall inside the door, and it ran from 1804 to 1998, and there were sixty-one names on it.

She had expected to be frightened by it. What she actually felt, working down it with her finger, was something more complicated and a great deal more useful: that all sixty-one had been men, and all sixty-one had been at work, and that the water in that harbour had never once taken anybody who was not being paid to be on it.

She recognised nineteen of the surnames. Four of them were in her class.

She came out and sat on the church steps for a while.

Then she went down to the harbour and looked at the shoe again, and it had completely changed, and she has never been able to explain to anybody how a thing changes when nothing about it has moved.

## Seventy Years Late

The woman came in the second week of September, on the ferry, with a stick.

Nél noticed her because she came down the harbour steps very slowly and then went and stood in front of the third bollard, which is what people did, and which meant something.

She stood there a long time. Longer than anybody Nél had ever watched.

Then she picked up the shoe.

Nél was up on the wall by the ice machine and she got down and went along, which she would not normally have done, and stopped about four metres off, which was as close as she could make herself go.

The woman said, without turning round: "You'll be wanting to know."

"Sorry."

"Don't be. If I were eleven and lived here I'd have been over quicker than that."

She turned the shoe over in her hands. She was perhaps eighty, and she was extremely well dressed, and she was not crying at all.

"It's mine," she said. "I was twenty-two months old. My mother had me on the wall in that pram there — well, not that one, but there — and she turned round for a matter of seconds and the pram went over the edge, brake and all."

Nél did not say anything.

"They got me out. Two men off a boat that shouldn't have been in that day. My mother could not tell the story without stopping in the middle, so she stopped telling it, and I grew up knowing it the way you know a thing that is never described." She held up the shoe. "One of these came off in the water. She looked for it, apparently. For years. She used to say it as a joke and it was not a joke."

"When was this?"

"Nineteen forty-eight."

The woman put the shoe back down on the third bollard.

Nél said, "Aren't you taking it?"

And the woman looked at her, and looked at the shoe, and said something that Nél has repeated to people ever since and has never once had come out right.

She said: "It's been doing a job."

Then she said that her mother had died in 1991 and had never known it had come back, and that she herself had only found out about the harbour and the bollard four months ago, from a cousin, at a funeral, in a conversation about something else entirely.

She stayed two nights and went back on the Thursday ferry.

Nél saw her once more, on the Wednesday evening, sitting outside the café with a coffee she was not drinking, looking at the water. She did not go over. She has wondered since whether she should have, and has concluded, most days, that she should not.

The shoe is still on the third bollard. It has been moved twice since — once into the café and once, briefly and outrageously, into a display at the tourist office — and both times somebody put it back.

Nél is grown now and she works at the harbour, and about four times a year, after weather, there is something on the third bollard, and she is one of the three or four people who quietly makes sure the right person gets told.

She has never seen the gull do it. Nobody has. She has asked everybody.

She has stopped thinking that this is the interesting part.
