## The Kite That Would Not Rise

The kite was good. That was the frustrating part.

If the kite had been bad, Dai would have known what to do. He would have taken it apart and started again and the whole thing would have been simple. But it was good, and he knew it was good, and it still would not fly.

He had made it in April from his father's instructions, which were written on the back of an envelope in his father's small square handwriting and which had been in the kitchen drawer for six years. The spars were bamboo out of the old window blind that had been in the shed since before they moved in. The sail was paper the colour of weak tea, glued along the folds and then glued again where the first glue had gone brittle. The bridle knots were the ones on the envelope, tied four times each until he got them right.

Every measurement was correct. He had checked them against the envelope twice and against a metre rule once.

It went up eight feet and came down like a dropped coat.

It did it thirteen times.

Not a spin, not a dive — nothing dramatic enough to be interesting. It went up, it hesitated, the nose came round, and it settled onto the grass with a small papery sound that Dai came to hate more than he had ever hated a sound.

The crows on the telephone wire watched all six.

There were seven of them. He noticed the number on about the fourth attempt, because they were in a row and evenly spaced, which crows are not usually, and because they had not moved. Crows on a wire shuffle. They open a wing and shut it. They shout at each other. They leave and come back.

These sat.

They watched the way a builder watches somebody else's wall — not unkindly, but with a professional attention that made Dai's ears go hot.

On the twelfth attempt, when the kite came down again in exactly the same way, the smallest of them made a sound.

It was not a caw. It was short and flat and came out of the side of its beak, and Dai understood it immediately and completely, with no evidence whatsoever, to be a criticism of his tail.

He stood in the middle of the field with the string in his hand and looked up at the wire and said, out loud, to seven birds:

"It's the length on the envelope."

The smallest one turned its head so that one eye was pointing at him, and said nothing at all, which was somehow much worse.

## Seven Crows and One String

He lengthened the tail that evening.

He did it in the kitchen with the envelope in front of him, feeling as though he were doing something slightly disloyal, and he added forty centimetres of torn sheet in bows, and he went out again the next day.

It went up thirty feet and stalled.

Thirty feet is better than eight. Thirty feet is a real height; at thirty feet the kite was above the hedge and you had to tip your head back. But it hung there and would not go higher and then it came down, tiredly, the way a thing comes down when it has been trying.

The crows watched. The small one said nothing.

He lengthened it again — another sixty, which made the tail longer than the kite by a distance he found embarrassing — and this time when he got to the field the small crow made no sound at all, which he decided, after some thought, to take as approval.

The trouble that afternoon was the wind.

It was the kind that arrives in gusts and then apologises. It would come across the field in a rush and flatten the grass in a moving line and the kite would leap, and then it would go, and there would be nothing at all for five or twelve seconds, and the kite would find itself standing on nothing.

That is not a thing you can fix with a tail.

Dai ran with it. He ran the length of the field four times, which is what you do, and which never works as well as it does in your head. By the fourth run the string had cut a line across his fingers and he was breathing in a way that would have embarrassed him if there had been anyone there.

There was, of course. There were seven of them.

They came off the wire together.

Not in a scatter. That was the thing he remembered longest — that they did not burst upward the way birds do when something startles them, all in different directions at once. They dropped off the wire in order, from the left, at intervals, like a sentence being read.

They came out across the field low and fast and rose under the kite, and they took the line.

Seven crows, at seven points along the string, each one closing its feet on it and beating steadily. They did not go to the kite. They went to the *string*, spaced along it like beads, and they held the whole length of it up out of the dead air.

And they did not lift the kite.

He was certain of this afterwards and he was certain of it at the time. They were not carrying it. They were showing it where the wind was — holding the line at the height where the moving air was, so that the kite could find it and lean on it.

It found it.

The kite went up so fast that the string burned through his fingers before he thought to let it. It went past the chimneys and past the church weathervane and kept going, and became a small brown shape against a very large white sky, and the crows fell away from the line one at a time and went back to the wire and sat down in a row.

Dai stood in the middle of the field with both arms shaking and the whole sky on the end of a piece of string.

## The Envelope in the Drawer

He asked his mother about the envelope that night.

He had been holding the question for a week and he asked it badly, over the washing up, in the way you ask a thing you have rehearsed too much: "Did Dad fly kites on the field, or somewhere else?"

She did not answer straight away. She finished the pan she was doing.

"The field," she said. "Every Sunday for about two years. He was insufferable about it."

"Was he any good?"

"He was terrible." She put the pan on the rack. "He was terrible for a very long time and then he was suddenly extremely good, and he could never explain what had changed, and it drove him mad. He used to say he'd had help." She said this lightly, and then, hearing herself, more carefully: "Why?"

"No reason."

She looked at him for slightly too long and then let it go, which he was grateful for.

He went out to the shed afterwards and got the envelope out of his coat and read it again under the light, and this time he read it properly instead of reading it for measurements.

The instructions were in his father's small square handwriting, and they were exactly what he had thought they were: spar lengths, bridle knots, the angle of the bow, the weight of paper. Numbers, mostly.

But at the bottom, under the last line, in the same hand but written faster, at a slight angle, as though added later and in a hurry, there was this:

*Tail is longer than the envelope says. Trust the wire.*

Dai stood in the shed with the door open and the moths coming in and read it five or twelve times.

*Trust the wire.*

He went out to the field the next afternoon at the same hour and they were there, all seven, evenly spaced, doing nothing. He stood underneath them with his hands in his pockets and looked up, and one by one they looked back at him with one eye each, which is how crows look at anything they are taking seriously.

"He was my dad," he said.

Nothing at all happened. A car went past on the top road. The smallest one shifted its feet on the wire and settled again.

He had not expected anything to happen. He had expected exactly this. And yet he stood there for a good while feeling foolish and then, gradually, less foolish, because the thing he had actually come to find out had already been answered before he opened his mouth — they were still here. Whoever they were and whatever they did, they had been on that wire for at least six years and probably a great deal longer, doing this, for whoever turned up on the field with a bad kite and a stubborn streak.

"He said you helped him," Dai said.

The smallest one made the short flat sound out of the side of its beak.

It was not, this time, a criticism of anything. He was almost sure of that. It was the sound you make when somebody says something obvious and you cannot be bothered to agree with it out loud.

He went home and put the envelope back in the kitchen drawer where it had lived for six years, and he did not tell his mother, and he has still not told her, although he thinks now that she probably knows, because she has never once asked him what happened to the ticket.

## The Message at the Top

His grandmother had told him, once, that you could send a note up a kite string.

She had told him in the front room in the winter, when he was small enough to be on the floor, and she had described it exactly: you write it on a scrap, you make a ring of it round the string, and you let it go, and the wind walks it up. All the way to the top. She said her brother had done it in Aberdaron in about 1951 and that it had taken most of an afternoon.

He had never had a kite that stayed up long enough to try.

He had the pen in his pocket already, which tells you something about what he had been hoping for.

He wrote it on the back of a bus ticket, holding the string under his arm, with the paper against his knee. It was not a wish. He wants that understood, because people always assume it was a wish, and there is something a bit cheap about a wish.

It was four words about his grandmother.

They are his, and he has never told anyone what they were, including the two people who have asked properly.

He curled the ticket into a ring and hooked it over the string and let go.

It went up in little hops. That was the part nobody had described to him. It did not travel smoothly; it caught, and stuck, and shivered, and then a gust would come along the line and it would run four or five metres and catch again. He stood with his head back and his neck aching and watched a bus ticket climb a piece of string for thirteen minutes.

He lost sight of it twice and found it again both times.

When it reached the kite — when it finally got up to where the string met the bridle, so far away that it was only a change in the shape of the thing — the crows let go.

All seven, at once, from the wire, and they did not fly. They simply dropped forward off the line and opened out and were gone in seven directions, low, over the hedge and the church and the allotments.

And the kite went on flying.

For about four seconds, with nothing holding the line and nobody pulling, in air that had gone completely still, the kite stayed exactly where it was.

Then it came down.

It came down slowly and in a long slant, across the field, over the wall, into the long grass by the water trough where the nettles are, and Dai walked the whole length of the field to get it with the slack string looping along the ground behind him.

It was not damaged. One spar had come out of its pocket, which took a minute to fix.

The ticket was gone.

He looked for it. He looked properly, for about half an hour, on his hands and knees in the long grass, and it was not there, and the string was unbroken from his hand to the bridle with nothing on it.

He flew that kite for four more summers. It is in his loft now, in a bag, with the envelope.

He never sent another one.
