## The Year the Tower Came Up

The reservoir went down in the drought and the church tower came out of it in the second week of August, and by the Friday there were cars parked all the way along the dam road.

Vera watched it happen from the caravan site with a mixture of feelings she has never entirely sorted out.

The tower was not much to look at. It was six metres of grey stone with no roof, standing in a plain of cracked mud the colour of a paper bag, and it had a tide mark round it at about the height of a person which was the mark of a normal year. People walked out to it across the mud, which they were not supposed to do, and stood next to it, and took photographs of themselves next to it, and drove home.

Vera worked at the caravan site in the holidays, cleaning the shower block and handing out the wristbands, and she heard the same four conversations every day for a month.

That it was sad. That it was fascinating. That they had seen a programme about one in Spain. And, from a man on the Thursday who was very pleased with himself, that it was a reminder that nothing lasts, which Vera thought was an odd thing to take from a village that had been deliberately drowned by people who filled in a form.

The newspaper called it *the ghost village*, which Vera's grandmother heard on the radio in the kitchen and responded to with a single syllable.

Her grandmother had been eleven when they flooded it. That was 1963. She had been eleven, and had left the house on the twelfth of March with a case and a bird in a cage, and had gone up the hill to the new houses at Aldeia Nova with everybody else, and had never gone back down because there was no back down to go to.

The new houses were fine. Vera had grown up in one of them. They had bathrooms and they had been built in a straight line facing the road, and every single one of them was identical, and her grandmother had lived in the same one for sixty-two years and had never once called it anything but *the new houses*.

She was seventy-four now and she lived with them and she had not been out to the dam road all week.

"They're calling it a ghost village," Vera said.

"Mm," said her grandmother.

"Have you been down?"

"No."

"Are you going to?"

Her grandmother put the pan down and looked out of the window at the hill for a moment, and Vera thought she was going to get an answer of some kind, and what she got instead was: "There's four hundred cars on that road and a man selling water out of a van. No."

"It's not, is it."

"It's a village," her grandmother said, "with a lot of water on it." She was doing something with beans. "Ghosts is what you say when you'd rather not say drowned, and drowned is what you say when you'd rather not say we did it on purpose for the electricity."

## The Eel

Vera found the eel in the third week, in one of the pools that were left in the mud, and she found it because she was doing the thing everybody at the caravan site was doing, which was walking about out there when the light went and it got cool enough.

The pool was maybe four metres across and knee-deep and warm as soup, and there were sticklebacks in it, and there was, along the bottom, an eel about as long as her arm.

She thought it was stuck. That was her first thought and it was a reasonable one — there were fish stranded all over that mud in August, dying in shrinking puddles, and she had already carried two carp to the main channel and been extremely pleased with herself.

The carp had been horrible to carry. They were enormous and they had gone quiet in the way a fish goes quiet, and one of them had come back to life halfway and nearly gone out of her arms, and she had walked the last thirty metres holding it against her chest like a baby with the mud sucking at her feet.

She had told nobody about the carp either. It was that sort of August.

She got down on her knees and put her hands in.

The water was the temperature of a bath that has been left. There was mud in it that came up in clouds the moment she moved, so that she had to wait between attempts for it to clear, kneeling in the warm with her sleeves pushed up and the sun going down behind the dam.

The eel did not want to be carried. It went round her wrists twice and got out the other side and came back to almost exactly the place it had started, and settled, and looked at her, which eels can do more than people expect.

She tried again and the same thing happened.

The third time she stopped and sat back on her heels in the mud and looked at where the pool was.

She was not thinking anything clever. She was thinking that she had been beaten by a fish and that her knees hurt.

It was in a straight line.

She had not noticed it because she had come at it from the side. But standing up, in the low sun, with the shadows long, she could see that the pool she was kneeling in was one of about nine, in a row, running away from her towards the tower — and that the row was not a row of pools at all.

It was a street.

The pools were the low bits. Between them, under a skin of dried mud, was the crown of a road, cambered, running from somewhere behind her all the way to the church.

She walked the length of it in the last of the light, out and back, four hundred paces one way, and where the crust was thin she could put her heel through it and there was stone underneath, laid stone, set on edge the way they used to set it.

## Learning the Way

She went out at the same time every evening for a fortnight and the eel was always in a pool on the street, and never the same pool.

That was the whole of the phenomenon and it does not sound like much written down. Vera would like to insist that it did not feel like not-much at the time.

She started marking. She used stones at first and then, when she worked out that people kicked stones, she used a stick to draw a line in the crust from pool to pool, and she extended it each evening from wherever the eel was.

The rule she gave herself, which she took extremely seriously, was that she was only allowed to draw as far as the eel had been. Not as far as she could see the low ground going. As far as the eel had actually been on an evening she had witnessed.

She has thought since that this was the only genuinely good decision she made that summer, and that she made it for no reason at all except that it felt like cheating otherwise.

By the end of the fortnight she had a line four hundred metres long, and it did not go where she expected.

She had assumed the road went from the dam road to the church, because that is the way people were walking. It did not. It came in from the north, along the flank of the slope, and made a wide turn about a hundred metres out that took it well away from the tower and then brought it back — a proper turn, the kind you make for a gradient, not for a boundary.

And it did not end at the church. It went past the church, on the north side, and there was a second line going off it at right angles that she found on the eleventh evening and that ran forty metres and stopped at a rectangle of stone about the size of a kitchen.

She drew all of it in a notebook, with the tower as the fixed point, pacing distances and writing them down.

She showed nobody for three weeks, because it was hers.

She did tell one person, on the second Saturday, which was a boy from the caravan site called Diogo who was there for a fortnight and who she thought would be interested.

He came out with her once, looked at a line drawn in mud, said it was cool, and asked whether there was anything to actually see. She said no, not really, and he was perfectly nice about it, and they went back and played cards.

That was the last time she told anybody until September.

She told her grandmother in the middle of September, in the kitchen, on an evening when the rain had finally started and everybody knew the pools were going.

She did not lead up to it. She put the notebook on the table and said, "This is the road into the village."

Her grandmother wiped her hands and sat down and pulled it towards her, and looked at it for a long time.

Then she put one finger on the wide turn.

"That's wrong," she said.

## One Turn

Vera, who had paced that turn four times and was twelve and had a notebook, said that it was not wrong.

"It's wrong," her grandmother said, "because you've got it going round the outside, and it went round the inside. There was a chestnut there. It went round the other side of the chestnut."

"There's nothing there now."

"No. There's a stump six metres down under two hundred million litres of water, and you didn't see it, and neither did the eel." She turned the notebook. "But it went round the inside. I've walked that road four thousand times."

And then she stopped, with her hand flat on the page, and did not say anything for a while.

Vera did not say anything either.

What her grandmother said, eventually, was: "I couldn't have told you that yesterday."

She said that she had not been able to get the village back for about ten years. That she could see the kitchen and she could see the front of the school and she could see two or three faces, and that everything in between had gone soft and would not come when she called it — and that this had frightened her more than she had told anybody, including Vera's mother, and that she had stopped trying because trying and failing was worse.

"And then you put a road in front of me," she said, "and I'm on it."

They did the rest of it over that winter.

Vera would put a question — what's here, what's at the end of this, why does it turn — and her grandmother would be somewhere in 1962 and would answer it, and about a third of the time she would answer a question Vera had not asked, and Vera learned quickly to write those down and not interrupt.

The map they finished in March has one hundred and nine things on it. Houses with names on them, which are the names of families and not of streets. The two shops. The place where the bus turned. A well that is not on any official plan. The chestnut, drawn as a circle, with the road going round the inside of it.

The reservoir filled that winter and the tower went under in January and has not been seen since.

Vera is twenty-eight and the map is in the parish office at Aldeia Nova, framed, next to the photographs, and there is a copy in the district archive because a woman from the archive came out and asked for one.

Her grandmother died in 2021.

The thing Vera says, when people ask her about it — and they do ask, because of the framed map, and because it is a good story about a drought and a fish — is that the eel did not know anything.

It was an eel in a pool. Eels lie up in pools. The pools were on the line of the road because the road was the low ground, and the road was the low ground because roads are built on ground you can walk on, and the whole of the mystery collapses the moment you look at it properly.

What the eel did was make her kneel down in the right place.

Everything else in that story was an old woman at a kitchen table who had been quietly losing something for ten years, and who got most of it back over one winter because a twelve-year-old asked her the questions in the right order — starting from the north, coming down the slope, going round the inside of the chestnut.
