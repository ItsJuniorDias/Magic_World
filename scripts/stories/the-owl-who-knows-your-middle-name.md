## The Landing

Block C had a landing on every floor with a window in it, and the window on the fourth floor did not shut properly, and this was the reason Nadia heard it and the reason she spent four months believing she was the only one who did.

The gap was about the width of two fingers at the top corner of the frame. In summer it was a good thing. In November it meant that the fourth-floor landing was the coldest place in the building and that you went up the last flight quickly.

She heard it for the first time on the ninth of November at about twenty past ten, coming up with the recycling, which was not a job she was supposed to be doing at twenty past ten and which she had volunteered for in order to be out of the flat for fourteen minutes.

Her brother had people round. That was the fourteen minutes. Her brother was seventeen and had reached the stage of having people round who filled the whole flat with a specific kind of loudness, and Nadia had discovered that the recycling took fourteen minutes if you did it slowly and that nobody ever questioned a person carrying a bag.

Something said her name.

Not her name. That is the whole of this story and it needs to be right the first time. It said the other one — the middle one, the one on her documents and nowhere else, the one that had been her grandmother's and her grandmother's grandmother's, three syllables long, with a sound in the middle of it that does not exist in the language she does her homework in.

It said it clearly, at a conversational volume, from somewhere just outside the gap in the window.

Nadia put the bag down on the landing and did not move for a considerable time.

Then she went up to the flat and put the chain on the door and sat on her bed with her coat still on.

Her brother's friends were still in the front room. Somebody was laughing at something on a phone. It went on being an entirely ordinary Tuesday on the other side of her bedroom wall for another two hours, and Nadia sat on the bed in her coat for most of it.

She had been at that school for twenty weeks. She had registered on the first day as Nadia K., because the form had a box for a middle name and she had left it empty, and the woman in the office had not asked. Nobody in that building, that school, or that city knew the middle name. Her mother knew it. Her brother knew it and had been paid, in a formal arrangement involving actual money, not to use it.

And something on the fourth-floor landing had said it out loud into an empty stairwell at twenty past ten at night.

The first three explanations she tried were all sensible and all failed within about a day.

That somebody had seen the documents — but the documents were in a folder in her mother's wardrobe and had not been out of it since the school registration in September.

That her brother had broken the arrangement — but he had not been in the building, he had been at football, and when she checked his phone location history two days later in a way she is not proud of, he had been at football.

That she had misheard something else. She gave this one four days and a great deal of effort, because it was the one she wanted. She sat on that landing and tried to make ordinary sounds turn into those three syllables. It cannot be done. The middle sound does not exist in this country and there is nothing in a stairwell that makes it.

## Why It Mattered

It is difficult to explain to an adult why a name is worth this much trouble, and Nadia has stopped trying.

The short version is that they had been in the country for two years and four months, and that the first eighteen of those had been in a different city, and that in the different city she had been herself and it had gone badly.

Not dramatically. Nobody had done anything to her that would go in a report. It had been six months of a particular kind of attention — of being asked to say it again, of watching a teacher decide not to attempt it in front of the register, of a boy in the year above who had made it into a noise and had done the noise, cheerfully, without malice, for most of a spring.

The worst of it was not the boy. The boy was straightforward and Nadia had known what to do about the boy by about the fourth week, which was nothing, and it had worn out on its own by June the way those things do.

The worst of it was the teachers who were kind about it.

There was a woman who taught geography who used to stop at Nadia's name in the register every single time, and take a small breath, and attempt it, and get it not quite right, and then smile at her — a warm, apologetic, completely well-meant smile that took about a second and a half and that happened in front of twenty-nine people, four times a week, for a year.

Nadia has thought about this a great deal since and has arrived at something she finds difficult to say out loud, which is that she would rather have had the boy.

She had arrived in the new city in September with a plan.

The plan was Nadia K. The plan was that Nadia is a name that exists in a dozen languages and belongs to nobody, that K. is a letter, and that there is no purchase on either of them.

The plan had worked. That is the part nobody ever wants to hear. It had worked completely. She had four people she ate lunch with and one she messaged in the evenings, and in twenty weeks not one person had asked her to say anything twice.

So when a voice on a landing said the other name, what she felt was not wonder.

She told her mother nothing. She would not have known how to start, and there was a second reason underneath the first, which was that her mother had cried about the name business exactly once, in the old city, in a car, and Nadia had decided that afternoon that it was never going to happen again on her account.

For three weeks she took the stairs two at a time past the fourth floor.

Then, on the second of December, she stopped on the landing on purpose, and stood by the gap in the window in the cold with her hands in her sleeves, and waited.

It took about four minutes.

It was very close — the other side of the glass, on the sill outside, where there was a ledge about a hand's width — and it said the name once and then made the other noise that owls make, the one that is not a hoot and that sounds like a hinge.

Nadia leaned over and looked, and there it was.

## Everybody Else

She found out about the rest of the building by accident, in the lift, from Mr Petrov on the sixth floor, who was about eighty and who had a way of starting conversations in the middle.

He said: "You have been standing on the fourth landing."

Nadia said she had been taking the recycling down.

"For twenty minutes. On Tuesday." The lift went past two. "It is all right. I have also stood there."

She said nothing at all.

The lift in Block C is slow and there are six floors and there is nowhere to look.

"Konstantin," said Mr Petrov. "Not Kostya. Not Kolya. Konstantin, the whole thing, which nobody has said in this country since my mother died in 1994." The lift stopped. "It gets it right, also. The stress in the correct place. Which the bank does not."

She rode up to six with him and then down to four and then up to six again, because he was talking.

What she found out over that week, and over the following month, by asking about fourteen people in a building of forty-four flats, was this.

The owl said everybody's.

Not everybody heard it — you had to be on a landing at night, and most people are not on landings at night, and a great many of the ones who are had put it down to a bird making a noise that sounded a bit like something.

But the ones who had heard it properly had all heard the same category of thing: the name that is not the one on the buzzer.

Mrs Óspina on two, who had been Mercedes Alejandra for sixty-one years and had been Mrs Óspina for thirty of them. A boy in the year above Nadia at school whose family had been in the country for four generations and whose middle name was his great-grandfather's and was, he said, "properly embarrassing," and who had heard it twice and told nobody until Nadia asked him directly in a corridor.

The building's caretaker, who said he did not believe in any of it and then, after a pause of about eight seconds, said his was Ferenc.

There were two people who had heard nothing and were annoyed about it.

And there was Mrs Deniz on five, who had heard it, and who had come out onto the landing with a broom, and who had been extremely clear with Nadia that she considered the whole thing an intrusion and that a name is private property and that she had written to the committee about the window.

Nadia thought about that one for a long time afterwards, because Mrs Deniz was not wrong. It is an intrusion. Something outside a window in the dark knows a thing about you that you have gone to considerable trouble to keep, and it says it out loud without being asked.

It only stops being an intrusion if you happen to be glad, and there is no version of the arrangement in which the owl checks first.

## What It Is For

Nadia asked Mr Petrov, in the end, what he thought the owl was doing.

He said that was the wrong question and that she should ask a better one, which annoyed her a great deal at the time and which she has since decided was the single most useful thing anybody said to her that year.

The better question took her until about February.

The better question was: *why does it only say the middle one?*

Because it does. Nobody in Block C has ever reported hearing their first name out of that window. Nadia has now asked, over nine years, something like thirty people, and the answer has never varied. It says the one that is not in use.

Which means — and this is the part she worked out on a landing in February with her hands in her sleeves, and which she has never had a better theory for since — that it is not identifying anybody.

If it wanted to identify her it would say Nadia. Nadia is what she answers to; Nadia is on the buzzer and the register and the library card. Saying the other one is, as an act of identification, useless.

It is doing the opposite thing. It is saying the part that is not being used, to the one person who knows it is not being used, in an empty stairwell, at night, where nobody else can hear.

Mr Petrov, when she put this to him in the lift in March, listened to all of it and then said only: "Yes."

"Yes what?"

"Yes, that is what it is doing." The lift went past four. "You wanted me to say whether it is a good thing. I am not going to. Mrs Deniz thinks it is not, and Mrs Deniz has buried two husbands and I have buried none, so I have stopped assuming I know."

She told her mother in April, in the kitchen, and did not manage it well, and her mother did not cry, and they sat there for a while afterwards not saying much.

Nadia is Nadia at work. She wants that clear, because people who hear this story sometimes want it to end with her changing everything back, and she did not, and she does not intend to, and she does not think that would have been braver.

What she did do, when she was nineteen, was write it — all three syllables, correctly, with the sound in the middle — on the inside cover of every notebook she owns, where nobody else looks.

And she lived in Block C until she was twenty-three, and for the last four of those years she went up to the fourth-floor landing perhaps once a month, at night, in the cold, and stood by the gap in the window until something on the ledge outside said it out loud in the right voice with the stress in the right place.

The window has been fixed now. She has been back twice and it shuts perfectly, and she has heard nothing either time, and she is entirely at peace about this and has told the building's committee that they did the right thing, which was a lie.
