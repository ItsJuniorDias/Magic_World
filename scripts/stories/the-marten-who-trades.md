## The Ledge

At Sveti Duh there is a ledge on the north wall of the church, about two metres up, where the buttress meets the stone, and it is about the size of a windowsill and it is where you put things out.

You put out four or five objects. Never one; one is not a trade, it is an offering, and an offering gets left exactly where you put it.

You put them out at dusk and you go away.

Going away is not optional and it is not superstition; anybody who has ever waited round that corner has got nothing, and the village's explanation for this is that she can hear you, which is almost certainly correct.

Nobody watches. There is no tradition of watching and there is no story in Sveti Duh about anybody having seen the exchange, and Jakob has never met a person who claimed to.

In the morning one of them is gone and there is something else in its place, and the something else is always better.

Not more valuable. Nobody in that village has ever got a coin off that ledge and Jakob's grandmother would tell you, at some length, about a man in the 1960s who put out a watch and a ring and two buttons in an attempt to establish an exchange rate, and who got back a piece of blue glass, and who was extremely annoyed about it for eleven years and who kept the glass until he died.

Better means better. That is the whole of the definition and there is not a better one available, and everybody in Sveti Duh finds this obvious and no visitor ever has.

What comes off that ledge, in Jakob's lifetime: a bootlace. A key. A plastic dinosaur. Four marbles that he knows of. A brass washer. A cigarette lighter that did not work. A piece of dark green sea glass from a coast three hundred kilometres away that caused a certain amount of discussion in 2014.

There is no pattern in the objects. The pattern, if there is one, is entirely in who got which, and it is not a pattern you can see from outside a family.

The marten is a pine marten and she lives in the roof of the barn behind the church and she has been seen perhaps thirty times in Jakob's life, mostly as a shape going along a wall at speed. She is not tame and she is not friendly and she has bitten one person, in 1998, who reached up onto the ledge while she was on it, which everybody agreed at the time was entirely his own fault.

Martens take shiny things. That is well documented and every naturalist Jakob has ever mentioned it to has said so within about a minute. They are curious, they cache, they will carry off a spoon, and there are people all over central Europe who have lost car keys to one.

The village has never disputed a word of that. The village's position, as Jakob's grandmother put it, is that knowing what an animal does is not the same as knowing what it is doing.

## What You May Not Do

The rule is that you may not choose what she takes.

You may choose what you put out — that is the whole of your part in it — but which of the four or five goes is not yours, and there is no known method of influencing it, and every single person in that village has tried.

Jakob's mother tried at fourteen by putting out four things she did not want and one she did, arranged so that the one she wanted was at the back.

It went.

His uncle tried by putting out five things he was equally indifferent to, on the theory that this was a fair test, and got nothing at all — everything was still on the ledge in the morning, which is what happens when there is nothing in the set worth taking, and which is the most common outcome and which nobody in Sveti Duh finds interesting.

About two-thirds of attempts come to nothing. Jakob's mother's estimate is that in a village of two hundred and forty there are perhaps eleven or twelve completed trades a year, and that most people in Sveti Duh do it three or four times in a lifetime and then stop.

Jakob's grandmother's position, which she stated once and would not elaborate on, was that the trade only works if there is something in it you would rather keep.

Jakob pushed her on it exactly once, at eleven, at her table, and got four more sentences than he expected.

She said that she had done it three times. She said that the first was at nineteen and that she had not understood what she was doing. She said that the second was in 1979 and that she had understood perfectly well and had done it anyway and had been right to.

He asked about the third and she said that it had not been taken, and that this had told her something, and that she had never gone back.

And that everybody knows which one that is, and that pretending otherwise is a waste of a dusk.

Jakob did not accept this at twelve and would like it recorded that he had reasons. It sounded, to him, like the kind of thing adults say to make a coincidence into a lesson. He had a plan and he thought the plan was good.

## The Knife

His father left in the March he was nine and there was nothing dramatic about it and Jakob has never described it as anything else.

He went to Austria for work, which about a third of that valley has done, and the arrangement was that he would come back at Christmas and he did, in 2019 and 2020, and then there was a period in which it was complicated, and then there was a woman in Graz, and by the time Jakob was twelve his father was a voice on a phone every second Sunday who was entirely pleasant and who talked about football.

Jakob's mother has never once said anything against him in Jakob's hearing, in eleven years, which he has recently begun to understand was not easy and was a decision she made early and stuck to.

Before he went he gave Jakob a knife.

It was a folding knife with a wooden handle and a brass bolster and it had been his own father's, and he gave it to Jakob in the kitchen on a Tuesday and said the things you say, and Jakob carried it every day for three years.

It was not a comfort. That is the part he could not have said at twelve and can say now without any difficulty at all.

It was a weight in a pocket that he checked for about forty times a day. It was a thing he could not use for anything, because using it would have wrecked it. It was an object that made every second Sunday harder rather than easier, and by the summer he was twelve he had reached a state in which he was angry with a piece of wood and brass and could not have explained why to anybody including himself.

He had taken it out at school in the June and a boy had asked whether he could see it and Jakob had said no, and had said it in a voice that made the whole table go quiet, and had thought about that for the rest of the term.

He went to the ledge in the August.

He went at dusk on the fourteenth, which is a Thursday in his memory and which the calendar says was a Sunday, and he has decided not to worry about that.

He put out five things. A good torch. A tin whistle. A knot of copper wire he had made and was pleased with. A fossil from the quarry. And the knife, at the front, in the middle, in the open, because he had worked out that the way to beat the rule was to stop hiding the thing.

His reasoning, at twelve, was that she took what you were protecting, so if he protected nothing she would have to take something else.

He has thought about that reasoning for eleven years and he still cannot find the flaw in it and it did not work.

## What She Left

The knife was gone and there was a marble in its place.

It was about two centimetres across and it was glass and it had a twist of blue and white through the middle and it was chipped in one place, and it was, by any measure anybody could apply, a completely worthless object.

Jakob was twelve and he stood at the north wall of that church at seven in the morning and cried, which he had not done about his father leaving and had not done about the Christmases and did not do again about anything for six years.

Then he put the marble in his pocket and went home and did not tell anybody for three years.

His mother worked it out inside a fortnight. She did not say anything about it for three years either, and when he finally told her she said, "I know," and put the kettle on, and that was the whole conversation.

He was furious about it for most of a year. He went back to the ledge eleven times that autumn with progressively worse ideas and never put anything out. He worked out four separate theories about how she had done it and one of them involved his mother.

Somewhere in the following spring it stopped mattering, in the ordinary way that things stop mattering, without an event.

Jakob is twenty-three. He is a joiner and he lives forty minutes down the valley and he sees his father about once a year in a way that is fine.

He has the marble. It is in a dish on a shelf with some screws.

His mother put it in that dish about six years ago, along with everything else that lives on that shelf, and it has been there since, and Jakob has never moved it into anywhere better and thinks that would be the wrong thing to do to it.

What he says about the whole business — and he does not say it often, and he said it once to his mother and once to a girl he was seeing and once, badly, to his father in a car — is that at twenty he realised he could no longer picture the knife.

He could tell you it was folding, and wooden, and had a brass bolster. He cannot see it. He tried for about a fortnight, deliberately, and got nothing but the words.

And he could draw that marble from memory in about four seconds, including which side the chip is on.

He does not think the marten knew any of that. He is quite clear that a pine marten is an animal that takes shiny objects and that there is a documented behaviour and that his village has built four hundred years of a very good social institution on top of an entirely ordinary mustelid.

What he says is only that he asked, at twelve, in the way you are allowed to ask, and that he tried to rig it, and that what he got back was worth less than what went and he would not trade it now for anything on that ledge.

He went back to the ledge once as an adult, at twenty-one, and put out five things, and thought about it for a fortnight beforehand the way you are supposed to.

Nothing was taken.

He stood there in the morning looking at his own five objects in the wet and worked out, on the way down, that this was the answer his grandmother had got at the third attempt and had never explained, and that he now knew what it told her, and that she had been right not to say.

He has not been back. He is not unhappy about it. It is, he thinks, a fairly good thing to be told about yourself at twenty-one by an animal that owes you nothing.
