## The Quiet Under the Hornbeams

There is a stand of hornbeam on the east side of the Bela valley where sound behaves oddly, and everybody in Podgora knows it and nobody thinks it is worth mentioning to visitors.

It is about two hundred metres of the path. You go in under the hornbeams and the valley noise stops — not muffled, stopped — and you walk through a silence that has a texture to it, and you come out the other side and the stream is there again as though it had been switched back on.

The Bela is a loud stream. It comes down over a bed of flat limestone and it can be heard from the road and from both sets of houses and, on a still night, from the church. It runs sixty metres from the hornbeam path along the whole of that stretch.

Under the hornbeams it is not there.

Iva had walked it four times a week for the whole of her life to get to the bus, and she had never once thought about it, in the way you do not think about the shape of the doorway in your own house.

She thought about it at twelve because of a school project on acoustics that she had chosen because it seemed easy.

The project was supposed to be about why the school hall was bad for concerts. Iva had chosen it on the Friday, had realised on the Sunday that she had nothing to say about the school hall, and had gone for the bus on the Monday morning through two hundred metres of silence she had walked through perhaps four thousand times.

She stopped in the middle of it and stood still and was late for the bus.

The books said dense vegetation absorbs sound and the books were right and also nowhere near enough. Absorption is gradual. You walk into a wood and it gets quieter over about forty metres. What happens under those hornbeams happens in about a metre and a half and it happens in both directions, and if you stand with your feet either side of the line and clap, the clap is a completely different object depending on which way you are facing.

Iva stood on that line for most of an afternoon in April with a stopwatch and a phone recording, and by the end of it she had established two things.

The line is not straight and it is not a line on the ground. It is a surface, roughly vertical, that runs across the path at a slight angle, and she found its position at four different heights by clapping and stepping and clapping again, and it leans.

The first was that the silence was not absorption.

The second was that it was worse in August than in April, and she only knew that because she had walked through it in August all her life and had a body that remembered.

That is not a measurement and she knows it. She had no April recording from any previous year and no August recording from that one, and the entire basis of the claim, at the time she made it, was that August under those hornbeams had always felt like being underwater and April did not.

She made the recordings that summer specifically to find out whether her body had been right about a thing it had never been asked about. It had.

## What Is In It

She found the web in the June, about four metres off the path, spun between two hornbeam trunks at head height, and she found it by walking into the edge of it and getting it across her mouth.

She was not looking for a web. She was looking for a surface — a rock face, a change in the ground, anything that could bounce or absorb — and she had been four metres off the path on the uphill side because that is where the trunks are thickest.

It is not a normal web and it is not a beautiful one. It is a rough sheet, perhaps two metres across, sagging in the middle, grey and slightly furred, with no radiating pattern at all — nothing like the wheel you get from a garden spider. Somebody who did not live there would take it for old cobweb and dust.

There is a spider. She is small and brown and unremarkable and Iva has seen her perhaps six times in eleven years, always at the top left, always still.

Iva has photographed her twice and shown the photographs to one entomologist, at a fair in Nova Gorica, who looked at them for a while and said it was probably a *Tegenaria* and that the web was atypical and that the photographs were not good enough, all of which was fair.

And the web catches sound.

Iva established this over that summer with a method she is still, at twenty-four, moderately pleased with. She clapped at measured distances and recorded. She did it in June, in July, in the first week of August, and in the last.

In June a clap from four metres came back off the recording thin but present.

By the end of August a clap from four metres was not on the recording at all. Not quiet — absent. The microphone got the click of her own jaw and the birds and no clap whatsoever.

She did it eleven times in the last week of August because she assumed the phone was faulty. She did it with a different phone. She got her cousin to clap while she held the phone, in case the problem was that the sound and the microphone were in the same place.

Nothing. Birds, wind, the stream from beyond the line, her cousin saying *now?* — which recorded perfectly well, because her cousin was standing outside the hornbeams — and then a silence exactly where the clap went.

The web was, by then, visibly loaded. It had gone from grey to a darker grey and it sagged about a hand's width lower in the middle and it did not move in the wind the way it had in June.

There is nothing in it. That has to be said, because everybody assumes there must be something visible. She has looked at that sheet from twenty centimetres with a torch in August and it is empty — no flies, no husks, no wrapped parcels. It is a grey sheet that has become heavier without acquiring anything.

That is the whole phenomenon and it is not, on its own, especially frightening.

The frightening part is what everybody in Podgora knows about September.

## September

In the second week of September the web comes down.

Iva has seen it happen twice and it takes about three days and it is not dramatic; the sheet detaches at the top corners and hangs, and then it is on the ground, and then it is not there.

The second time she watched for it deliberately, going up every evening from the first of the month, and she still missed the actual detaching on both corners, which happened, both times, in the night. Nobody in the village has ever seen the spider do it and nobody has ever seen her spin the new one either, which starts appearing in the last week of March.

And for about six days after it comes down, the hornbeams give it back.

It starts on the evening of the day the sheet reaches the ground and it is over by the seventh morning, and in between it is continuous. It does not stop at night. Iva went up at two in the morning once, at seventeen, on a bet, and it was going then at the same volume with the same wash.

You walk through and you hear voices.

They are not loud. They are at the volume of a conversation two rooms away, and they overlap completely — hundreds of them, all at once, in no order, men and women and children and a dog and once, unmistakably, a chainsaw. It is a wash. There is no way to pick anything out of it. It sounds, Iva has always thought, exactly like a school corridor at the end of a lesson, heard from a stairwell.

That is everything anybody said under those hornbeams between March and August, coming back out of the trees over six days in September, all at the same time.

Nobody in Podgora finds this alarming. They find it a nuisance, in the way you would find a road being resurfaced a nuisance — for six days the path is unpleasant to walk and the dogs will not go up it, and then it stops.

The village has a phrase for that week. It translates roughly as *the trees are talking* and it is used the way you would use *the leaves are down* — as a marker in the year, mildly, with no weight on it at all. Iva's mother says it every September and has never in her life given it a second thought.

Iva asked her once whether it had ever bothered her.

Her mother said that when she was small she and her brother used to go up there and shout into it, to see if they could hear themselves come back the following year, and that they never could, and that they had got bored of it by about the age of ten.

Iva found it extremely alarming in the September she was twelve, and the reason is that in April she had said something on that path.

## What She Said

She had said it to Marta, at the far end of the hornbeams, in the second week of April, and she had said it in a temper, and it had been about Marta's father.

She is not going to write down what it was. It was not a secret and it was not a lie; it was a true thing about a difficult situation that everybody in the village knew and nobody in the village said, and Iva had said it, out loud, at some volume, to the one person on earth who should not have had to hear it.

Marta had gone home. They had made it up by about the following Tuesday in the way twelve-year-olds do, which is to say entirely and without ever referring to it.

And then in June Iva had found a web, and by August she had worked out what it did, and by the first of September she had spent three weeks understanding that in about ten days the entire village was going to walk up that path through a wash of everything said under those trees since March.

She did not sleep well. She considered, seriously, going up there and taking the web down early, and she got as far as the third hornbeam with a stick before she worked out that she had no idea whether that would empty it slowly or all at once and that all at once was the thing she was trying to avoid.

She went up on the first evening of it because not going was worse.

And it was a wash.

She stood in the middle of the hornbeams for eleven minutes with her hood down and she could not pick out one word. Not one. There were voices and voices and voices and every single one of them was inside every other one, and the whole thing had the acoustic character of a swimming pool.

She went up on all six evenings.

On the last one, walking out at the north end, with the noise thinning behind her, she heard one thing clearly.

It was three words and it was not hers and it was not Marta's, and it was in a voice she recognised immediately, and it was about something that had nothing to do with her at all.

Iva is twenty-four.

She has never told anybody what those three words were or whose voice it was, and the person is still alive and lives four hundred metres from her mother's house, and Iva has been perfectly ordinary with them for twelve years.

What she has said, to two people, is the other half of it — the part she worked out on the walk down that night and has not improved on since.

She had spent five months terrified that a wood was going to say her worst sentence out loud in front of everybody. And the wood had said it, on all six evenings, along with about nine thousand other sentences, and it had been indistinguishable, and nobody had heard a thing.

And then on the last evening it had handed her, for free, three words that were somebody else's, and she has had to carry them ever since.
