## One Night in October

There was a night in October when the geese went over Szentmárton, and it was the only night of the year that anybody in that village stayed up on purpose.

They came late — never before eleven, usually nearer one — and they came low. Lower than geese have any business flying. Bori's father said you could hear the wingbeats, which she had assumed for years was an exaggeration, and then had heard them at nine years old and had never argued about it again. It is a sound like a room full of people shaking out sheets, and it goes on for a long time, and it goes over the roofs at a height that makes the dogs mad.

You did not go out and watch. That was the first rule and nobody had ever explained it and everybody kept it.

Bori had asked about that one more times than about anything else, and the answers were all versions of the same shrug. Her father said you just didn't. Her mother said it was rude. The priest said, in a slightly evasive way, that it was a matter of respecting privacy, and did not specify whose.

The only answer that ever satisfied her came from old Feri at the workshop, who said: "Because you'd look."

"That's the same thing."

"No it's not. You'd look, and you'd see whose is whose, and you'd see which windowsill's got one on it and which hasn't." He went back to the lathe. "It's not the geese we're being polite to."

You put your letter out before dark and you went in and you shut the door.

The letters were not letters in any sense a postman would recognise. There was no envelope, no name, no address, and above all no signature — a signed one, said Bori's grandmother, is worse than useless, and the one time somebody had signed one in her lifetime the whole business had gone badly for a family that was no longer in the village to be embarrassed about it.

You wrote the thing you could not say.

That was the entire specification. Not a wish. Not a complaint. Not a prayer, and the priest had been quite clear about that from the pulpit in 1988 and had privately put one out himself in 1991, which everybody knew and nobody mentioned.

The thing you could not say. To the person you could not say it to. In as many or as few words as it took.

Then you folded it once and put it somewhere outside — a windowsill, a fence post, the hollow in the wall by the chapel, wedged in a hedge — and you went indoors and did not look out.

In the morning they were gone.

Bori had tested this once, at ten, in a spirit of pure enquiry, and would like it recorded that she did not put out a real one to do it. She wrote *this is not a real letter* on a piece of paper, folded it once, and put it on the sill.

It was there in the morning, damp and perfectly ordinary, and she took it in and felt obscurely told off.

Not blown about the lane, not soaked in the ditch, not caught in the fence. Gone, all of them, every year, from a village of two hundred and six people in a country where the wind in October takes anything not nailed down.

## What Happens To Them

Bori's understanding of the arrangement, at twelve, came mostly from her grandmother, who had put one out every year since 1961 and who talked about it the way other people talk about the gas bill.

The most important thing — the thing that took Bori longest to accept, and that she got wrong at first the way everybody gets it wrong at first — was that the letters are not delivered.

Nobody receives one. Ever. There is no story in Szentmárton about a woman in Debrecen who found a piece of paper in her yard, and no story about anybody learning anything they did not know. Bori went looking for such a story on purpose, at seven, asking seven adults, and got nine blank looks and one raised eyebrow.

They are taken away.

Bori's first version of the arrangement, at about eight, had been the obvious one: that the geese were postmen, and that somewhere there were people opening things. She had liked that version. It had a shape.

She had built quite a lot on it before her grandmother took it apart in about four sentences one afternoon, and she remembers being genuinely upset, and she remembers her grandmother being entirely unsympathetic about the upset.

That is what the geese do. They come over low on one night in October and they take the year's unsaid things out of the village, and where they go with them is not anybody's business, and Bori's grandmother had views about people who asked.

"But then what's the point?"

Her grandmother was mending something and did not look up. "Say it out loud, then. Say the thing to the person. Go on."

Bori said that was not fair.

"It isn't fair. I know exactly what's in mine and I've known for sixty-four years and I'd sooner have my hand in the fire than say it in this kitchen." She bit the thread. "But I can put it down. Once a year I can put it down and it goes somewhere and it is not in this house any more, and in the morning I can look at your grandfather across that table."

Bori did not have anything to say to that.

"It isn't a solution, kislány. It's a place to put a thing. There aren't many, and they're worth knowing about."

Bori asked her, once, and only once, whether it was the same thing every year.

Her grandmother put the mending down, which she did not do, and looked at the window for a while.

"For about thirteen years it was," she said. "Then it stopped being, and then for a long time it was small things, and now for the last few years it's been the same one again, but it's not the same one it was at the start. It's the other end of it."

That was the entire conversation and Bori has thought about it for twenty years.

There was one more rule, which was not a rule so much as a fact everybody had learned by observation.

Once it is out, it is out. You do not go and get it back.

Not because anything happens to you if you do. Nothing happens to you. Bori's uncle had gone out and retrieved his in 2009 at about half past ten, having thought better of it, and had come in with the paper in his hand and had been perfectly fine and had burnt it in the stove.

But he had not put one out since, and he was not the same at that kitchen table, and Bori's grandmother had said one sentence about it in fifteen years, which was: *he's still carrying it.*

## Three Days Early

Bori wrote hers on the eighteenth of October in a temper, at half past nine at night, sitting on her bed with the door shut.

It was about Emese.

Emese had been her friend since they were four and had started at the gimnázium in Kecskemét in September, which meant the bus at ten past six and back at seven, and which meant that Emese now had a whole life seven hours a day that Bori was not in. That was not the problem. Bori had understood about the bus.

The problem was a thing Emese had said on the Sunday, at the shop, in front of two girls from Kecskemét, which was not cruel and was not even untrue and which had been said in a particular voice — a slightly amused, slightly explaining voice — and which had made Bori, for about four seconds, into somebody being described rather than somebody standing there.

Emese had almost certainly not noticed doing it. That was the worst part.

Bori had watched her not notice. That was the thing she could not get past: she had watched Emese's face for the whole four seconds, hoping to find something in it, and there had been nothing there at all — just Emese, being funny in a shop, in a good mood, with two new people she liked. Bori had gone over it about two hundred times by Wednesday and was completely certain that if she said anything, Emese would be baffled, and would apologise, and would mean it, and would not understand, and then Bori would have spent the thing and got nothing.

So she wrote it.

She wrote about a page. She was not proud of the middle section then and is not now. She said the thing about the voice, and then she said several other things that had been sitting underneath it since about March, and one of them was true and specific and would have ended it between them for good.

She folded it once and went out in the dark and put it in the hollow in the wall by the chapel, and came back in and slept better than she had in a fortnight.

That is the part she wants understood. She slept well. She was not tormented; she did not lie awake regretting it. She had done the thing you are supposed to do with an unsayable thing, and she had done it properly, and she had felt the exact relief her grandmother had described.

The date was the eighteenth. The geese came on the twenty-first, every year, give or take a day. She had three days.

The geese came that night.

They were three days early. They had not been three days early in living memory — there was an argument about 1974 that nobody could settle — and Bori woke at some time after midnight to the dogs going and the sound of a room full of people shaking out sheets, going over low, for a long time.

She sat up in bed in the dark with her heart hammering and understood, before she was properly awake, exactly what she had done.

She got as far as the top of the stairs.

The rule about not going out is not enforced by anything. There is no penalty and there is no watchman and her parents were asleep. She stood at the top of the stairs in the dark for the length of the whole flight-over, with her hand on the rail, listening to the wingbeats going over the roof, and she did not go down.

She has never been able to decide whether that was obedience or cowardice or the first genuinely adult decision of her life, and she suspects it was all three and that they are frequently the same thing.

## What Was Left To Say

She went to the chapel wall at first light and the hollow was empty and there was frost on the stones.

She stood there for a while in the cold doing the only useful thing available, which was working out precisely what she had lost.

It was not that Emese would read it. Nobody reads them. She knew that, she had always known that, and she made herself say it out loud on the road to be sure she believed it: *nobody is going to read it.*

What she had lost was the sentence.

She had put down, three days early, in a temper, the true and specific thing that would have ended it — and it had gone, and it was not available to her any more. She could remember writing it. She could not, standing at that wall in the frost, actually get hold of the sentence. It had the shape of a word you cannot find, and it stayed like that for the rest of her life, and she has never once been able to reconstruct it.

Which left her with the other thing. The small one. The one about the voice at the shop.

She said it to Emese on the Saturday, at the bus stop, badly, in about four sentences, looking at the timetable and not at Emese.

Emese was baffled, and apologised, and meant it, and did not understand.

And then, about a week later, said — out of nowhere, in the middle of something else entirely — "I do do that. The thing you said. I did it to Réka on Tuesday and I heard myself."

They are thirty-one now. Emese lives in Budapest and Bori does not, and they speak most weeks, and they have had one serious falling-out in the whole of that time and it was about something else.

Bori has put a letter out every October since she was fifteen.

She has never once written in a temper again — she writes hers in the first week of the month now, in daylight, at the kitchen table, and takes several days over it — and she has never gone out to retrieve one, and she has never told anybody in her family what is in them.

She has thought a great deal about what her grandmother said, and she has an addition to it that she has not said to anybody either.

Her grandmother said it was a place to put a thing.

Bori thinks it is also a way of finding out which thing was the real one. You can only put out one. You sit down with everything you have been carrying, and you have to choose, and the choosing is the whole of it — because whatever you write down is going away for good, and whatever you do not write down is the thing you are going to have to say out loud to somebody at a bus stop.
