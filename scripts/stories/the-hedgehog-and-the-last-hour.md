## The Hour That Is Not There

Rui found out about it because of a maths test.

The test was on a Friday and he had meant to work on the Thursday evening, and on the Friday morning he had not worked on it, and the reason he had not worked on it was that he could not account for the Thursday evening at all.

He could account for the afternoon. He had come in at twenty past four, and there had been the business with the bicycle chain in the yard, and his aunt had rung and been on the phone about the wedding for a while. He could account for the night: he had been in bed at half nine and had read for a bit and had heard the ten o'clock news through the floor.

Between those two there was nothing.

Not a blur. Not the ordinary vagueness of an evening where you did not do much. Nothing at all, in the way that there is nothing before you were born.

He sat in the maths test working out what he could do about the four questions he had not revised, and underneath that he was thinking: *where did Thursday go.*

It happened again on the Sunday.

He noticed it the moment it happened, or rather the moment after — he was at the kitchen table with the light on and it was properly dark outside and the last thing he was certain of was standing at the back door looking at the sky going orange behind the depot.

He got up and went to the door and opened it and stood there. It was cold. It was black. The depot lights were on. There was no orange anywhere.

He went and asked his mother what time it was and she said quarter past seven without looking up, and Rui stood in the kitchen doorway holding on to the frame and doing arithmetic that would not come out.

He had lost an hour and ten minutes and he had lost it while awake, standing up, at a back door.

Over the following fortnight he kept a record, which is what he did with things. He wrote down the last moment he was sure of and the first moment after, and by the end of November he had nine entries and they had a shape.

It was always the same hour.

Not the same clock time — the clock time moved, earlier and earlier as the month went on. It was the hour when the light goes. The last of it, the part where the sky has colour in it and the streetlights come on and everything outdoors turns the colour of a photograph of itself.

That hour, a dozen times, had not happened to him.

He asked his mother, carefully, whether he had been strange on the Sunday.

She said he had been perfectly normal, that he had helped with the potatoes and had been quite chatty about a boy at school called Duarte, and that she did not know why he was asking, and could he not stand in the doorway with the cold coming in.

## The Box Under the Hedge

He found the box on the eleventh of December, under the hedge at the bottom of the yard, and he found it by putting his hand somewhere he should not have put his hand.

The hedge at the bottom was thick at the base and full of the things hedges are full of. He was after a ball that had gone under, and he was flat on the wet ground with his arm in up to the shoulder, and his fingers came down on a corner that was flat and dry and cardboard.

It took him a long time to get it out. It was further in than his arm went and he had to fetch the rake and work it sideways.

While he was working it out he found four other things, which is what happens under a hedge: half a tennis ball, a fork, a plastic dinosaur that had been his when he was about six, and a great quantity of leaves that had been leaves for a very long time.

None of them were dry.

It was a shoebox. Green, with a brand on the side that had not existed for twenty years, and it was not damp, which under a hedge in December is not a thing a shoebox can be.

He sat back on his heels in the wet and took the lid off.

The box was full of evenings.

He has never worked out how to describe this and he has stopped trying, so what he says now is simply what he saw, which is that the box contained nine flat pieces of light about the size of a postcard, lying on top of each other like slides, and every one of them was the last hour of a day.

You could see into them. Not a picture — a picture is flat and this was not flat, it had a distance in it. He looked into the top one and there was the back yard from the height of his own eyes and the sky going orange behind the depot and the sound, faintly, of his aunt on the telephone in another room.

The second one down was a different evening. There was rain on it.

He went through all nine, kneeling in the mud with the lid beside him, holding each one up to what was left of the daylight.

Three of them had rain. One had the smell of somebody burning garden waste two gardens over, which he remembered and had not thought about since. One was almost entirely dark and had a single lit window in the top corner, which he worked out after a while was the Ferreiras' landing.

And every one of them was the yard. Every single one was that view, from that height, from the back door — as though somebody had stood in exactly his place on fourteen separate evenings and had not moved.

He put the lid back on because his hands had started to shake, and he sat in the mud at the bottom of the yard with a shoebox on his knees for a while, and then he heard the noise.

It was a snuffling, industrious, entirely un-mysterious noise, coming from under the hedge about a metre to his left, and it was the noise of a hedgehog who has been interrupted and is deciding whether to be bothered about it.

## What She Was For

She was not enormous and she was not small and she did not, when he finally got a proper look at her, seem in the least surprised.

She had come out and stopped about half a metre from his knee and turned herself broadside on, which he later learned is what they do when they are considering the situation rather than fleeing it.

She was not what he had pictured, which was a Christmas card. She was bigger than a Christmas card and much dirtier, and there was a patch on her left side where the spines were thin, and she smelled — this is the detail he always includes and people never like — she smelled strongly of a hedge.

They looked at each other for a while. A dog barked over towards the depot and neither of them reacted to it.

Rui said, "This is mine."

The hedgehog did not say anything, because hedgehogs do not, and Rui would like that understood at the outset because people always assume this story has a talking animal in it and it does not.

What she did was go back under the hedge and come out again with something in her mouth.

It was another one. Flat, about postcard size, with a dark blue evening in it and a smell of frost coming off it that he could feel on his face from where he sat.

It was not from the box. That was the thing he understood immediately and could not have explained. The box was on his knees with the lid on. She had gone back under the hedge and had come out with a twelfth one from somewhere else, and he never found out where, and he did ask, later, in the only way available to him, which was to look under the hedge with a torch for about an hour.

She put it down on the ground in front of him, and then pushed it towards him with her nose, twice, in a way that could not have meant anything else.

He picked it up.

And that is how Rui found out what the last hour of a day feels like when it is handed back to you all at once instead of being lived through a minute at a time.

It was the eighth of October. He knew it was the eighth of October because he was in it. He was standing at the back door — the same back door — and the sky was that colour, and inside the house behind him his grandmother's carers were in the hall talking to his mother in the low voices that adults use when they think they are being quiet.

And Rui, standing at the back door on the eighth of October, was eleven years old and was not going to cry, and had gone out into the cold specifically so that he would not, and was doing the thing where you press your thumbnail hard into your finger.

The hour ran through him and finished and he was back in the mud in December with a piece of cardboard in his hand that was no longer anything at all.

It had lasted, by the clock, no time whatsoever. He checked. He had gone in and it had been twenty to five and he came out of it and it was twenty to five, and the light at the bottom of the yard had not changed.

It had also lasted an hour and ten minutes, all of it, in order, including the part in the middle where nothing happened and he had just stood there being cold.

He looked at the hedgehog.

The hedgehog looked back at him with the total lack of embarrassment of a creature that has done nothing wrong.

## Nine Evenings

His grandmother had been ill from the middle of September to the middle of November and had then, slowly and grumpily, got better, and was at that time upstairs complaining about the television.

There were nine evenings in the box and there had been nine entries in his notebook, and the dates were the dates.

Rui did the sum in about four seconds and then sat with it for most of a week.

She had not taken his afternoons. She had not taken the nights, or the mornings, or the two whole days in October when it had been bad and he had known it was bad. She had taken exactly the hour when the light goes — the hour when you are eleven, and there are people in the hall, and nobody has told you anything, and it is not yet time for bed.

That hour, a dozen times.

He thought for a while that he should be angry, and tried it, and it did not fit.

What he settled on instead — and he was eleven, and it took him until about February to put it in these words — was that somebody had looked at him standing at a back door pressing his thumbnail into his finger and had decided that he did not have to carry that particular hour on that particular night, and had taken it, and had not thrown it away.

That was the part. She had not thrown it away.

She had put it in a box under a hedge four metres from his own back door and kept it dry for three months.

He took them back one at a time.

He did it over the winter, out at the bottom of the yard in the cold, roughly one a week, and he chose which one by looking into it first, which she permitted. Some of them were nothing much. Two of them were extremely bad. The one from the twenty-ninth of October he put back in the box three times before he took it, and when he did take it he sat under the hedge afterwards for twenty minutes.

He finished the last one in March.

He put the empty box back under the hedge, because it was hers and not his, and it is still there as far as he knows.

Rui is nineteen now. His grandmother lived another five years and was extremely rude about the television for all of them.

He has not lost an hour since, and he is not sure whether that is because nothing has been bad enough, or because you only get that arrangement once, or because it is not an arrangement at all and there is simply a hedgehog at the bottom of a yard in a town in the north who notices things and does what she can with them.

He leaves food out. He has read the guidance and he leaves the correct food and not the food people used to leave.

He has never once seen her take it, which he finds entirely in character.
