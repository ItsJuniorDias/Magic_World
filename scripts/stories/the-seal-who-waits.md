## Once Each

The rule about the seal was not written down anywhere and everybody in the bay knew it exactly.

You saw her once. Not once a year, not once a season — once, in a life, and then never again, and the once was not at a time of your choosing. And you did not go looking, because looking did not work, and everybody had an uncle who had spent a summer proving it.

Maeve knew the rule the way she knew that the harbour steps were slippy on the fourth one from the bottom. She had never been told it in a single sitting. It had assembled itself out of twelve years of overheard sentences at the co-op and on the pier and in the back of the van.

What she had noticed, and what nobody talked about, was the shape of when it happened.

Mr Nolan had seen her the winter he lost the *Bright Star*, three days before the insurance came through. The Coyle brothers had both seen her, separately, in the same week, which was the week their mother was in Sligo hospital. The woman who ran the chandlery had seen her on the morning she had decided to sell the chandlery, and had not sold it.

Maeve made a list once, in a maths copybook, of the thirteen sightings she could get people to be specific about. Then she wrote what had been going on for that person at the time, which took longer and involved asking questions that made two adults change the subject.

She looked at the list for a while.

Then she wrote, at the bottom, in small letters: *she comes when you are about to stop.*

She did not show it to anyone. It is still in the copybook and the copybook is still in the drawer under the stairs.

There were two things about the list that she did not write down.

The first was that in every single case, the person had carried on. Mr Nolan had bought the second boat, which everyone agreed was a worse boat. The Coyles had kept fishing through the whole of their mother's illness and after it. The chandlery was still there, with the same woman in it, still complaining about the same suppliers.

That could have meant that the seal changed something.

It could also have meant that she only came to people who were going to carry on anyway, and that the ones who really stopped never saw her, and that nobody had ever thought to count those because a person who leaves a place stops appearing in the conversations of that place. There was no list of the people who had gone. There could not be. That was the whole difficulty with it.

Maeve turned this over for most of a winter and could not find a way to test it, and it bothered her in a way she has never entirely got rid of.

The second thing was smaller and worse.

Her father had seen the seal. Everybody knew he had. It was said about him the way you say a man is left-handed — a settled fact, mentioned in passing, of no particular weight.

And in twelve years nobody had ever once said when.

## Her Father's Year

Her father had seen the seal and would not discuss it.

This was unusual, because her father discussed everything. He was a man who could take twenty minutes to explain a decision about diesel. But if the seal came up — and it came up, because it came up in that bay the way the weather comes up — he would go quiet and busy, and find something that needed doing with his hands, and the conversation would move on without him.

Maeve worked out the year from other people.

It had been the year before she was born. The boat he had then had gone on the rocks at the north end in a February gale, with nobody aboard, because he had not gone out that morning, and the not going out was the part nobody explained properly. He had lost the boat and the gear and, for nine months, the licence.

She asked her grandmother about it once, in the kitchen, when they were podding peas.

Her grandmother did not look up. "He'd made his mind up," she said.

"To do what?"

"To go to Liverpool. He had a cousin there and a job at the end of it. He'd told me and he'd told your mother and he'd gone down to the pier that evening to look at the water and say goodbye to it, which is a thing men here do and pretend they don't."

She pushed a pod open with her thumb.

"He was twenty-four and he'd been on the water since he was younger than you and there was nothing left in this bay for him. That's not a mood, love. That's a sum. He did the sum and the sum came out Liverpool."

She was quiet for a moment. Peas went into the bowl.

"I'd have gone with him. I want you to know that, because you'll hear it told differently by people who weren't in the house. I had the case down off the wardrobe. It was not a bad decision and I'll not sit at this table and have anyone call it one."

"So what changed it?"

"I've told you. He went down to look at the water."

"That's not an answer, Avoa."

Her grandmother looked at her then, properly, over the bowl.

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

"And he came back up an hour later and he never mentioned Liverpool again."

Maeve waited.

"That's the whole of it, love. I've never had another word out of him about that evening and I've stopped asking. Some things a person needs to keep whole."

## The Long Week

Her grandmother had thirteen creels in the water off the point, and had had them there for forty years, and in April she had a stroke.

It was a small one, as those things go. She came home after nine days and she could walk with a stick and she was extremely irritated about the stick. But her hands did not do what she asked them to any more, and there was no version of the situation in which she was going out to the point in a punt.

So Maeve went.

She was eleven, which everyone had opinions about, and the arrangement was that she went with her father when he was in and with Mr Nolan when he was not, and that she never, under any circumstances, went alone.

She hauled thirteen creels twice a week from April to July.

It was heavier than she had understood. That was the thing that surprised her — not the cold and not the early mornings, which she had been warned about, but the sheer physical arithmetic of a creel full of water coming up over a gunwale. She got better at it. She got a system. She got a place on her right palm that stopped being a blister and started being something else.

The system was this.

You came at the creel from downtide, so the punt held still against the rope instead of swinging off it. You took the buoy with the hook and never with your hand, because your hand would be wet and cold and would not close properly by the fourth one. You brought the creel up in three pulls with a rest between, and on the rest you put your forearm along the gunwale and let the wood take the weight instead of your back — which her grandmother had taught her without a single word, by putting one hand on her shoulder and pressing down until Maeve moved her arm to the right place herself.

And you did not look into the creel until it was aboard.

That was the important one. Looking early made you hurry, and hurrying was how a rope went over the side, and a rope over the side in April water was how a girl of eleven became a thing that happened in that bay.

Thirteen creels took about two and a half hours with Mr Nolan, who talked the entire time about a dispute over a mooring that had begun in 1998. It took about ninety minutes with her father, who did not talk at all, and who watched her do the third pull on every single creel without ever once offering to take it.

Her grandmother asked about them every evening, in the same words, from the chair by the window.

*What were they like.*

And Maeve would go through them in order — this one heavy, this one empty, weed on the north two, a crab in the fifth that was undersized and went back — and her grandmother would listen with her eyes shut and say *mm* in the places where *mm* went. Sometimes she would say *that's the ground there* or *she'll fill next week*, and once she said *your grandfather lost that one twice*, about a creel that was in fact new.

That quarter of an hour was the part of the day that Maeve did not mind at all, and she understood at the time that she was going to remember it, which almost never happens while a thing is still going on.

And in the second week of July she decided she was going to stop.

She decided it on a Tuesday, rowing back in, with an empty box and a low sky and her hands hurting in a new way. It was not dramatic. She was not crying. She had simply run the numbers, the way you do at eleven when nobody is watching, and the numbers said: her grandmother was not going to be able to do this again, and her father could not do this and his own boat, and Mr Nolan was seventy-one, and she could not do it alone for the rest of her life, and every week she kept it going was a week that made the ending harder for everybody.

The kindest thing, she thought, was to stop now while it was still her doing it.

She was going to say it that evening at the table. She had the sentence ready. She had been building it since the point.

She rowed in past the black buoy with the sentence in her mouth.

## Not Yet

The seal came up six feet off the port bow and looked at her.

Maeve stopped rowing.

She was grey and she was enormous — much bigger than Maeve had pictured, and she had pictured her often — and she had a scar across the bridge of her nose that had gone pale. The water ran off the top of her head. She was low in the water, only the head and a little of the shoulder, and she was completely still, and she looked at Maeve with round black eyes that were not kind and not unkind and not anything, and were paying complete attention.

They stayed like that for a length of time that Maeve has never been able to estimate. She has said thirty seconds. She has said four minutes. She does not know.

Nothing happened.

That is the part she has never been able to explain to anybody, including herself. There was no sign. Nothing was communicated. The seal did not nod, or gesture, or swim in a meaningful direction, or do any of the things that would have made it a story with a shape.

She looked at Maeve, in the flat grey light, with the oars up and the water ticking under the hull, until Maeve understood that she was being looked at properly — the way almost nobody looks at an eleven year old — and then she went down without a splash and did not come up again.

Maeve sat in the punt for a while.

Then she rowed in, and tied up, and carried the empty box up the steps, and went home, and had her dinner, and did not say the sentence.

She has never worked out what happened. She has considered every version of it. That the seal comes to stop you. That the seal comes because you are about to stop and the stopping is the thing that calls her, and she has no opinion about it whatsoever, and just comes to look, the way you look at a fire. That there is no seal and there is only an ordinary grey seal in an ordinary bay and seven people over sixty years happening to be at the end of their rope on the day they saw her.

She thinks the last one is probably true and she does not think it makes any difference.

Because the thing that actually happened, the thing she can point to, was this: for four minutes on a Tuesday in July, at the exact moment when she had decided that what she was doing did not count, something came up out of the water and gave her its whole attention.

She kept the creels going until September, when the season ended, which was always when it was going to end.

Her grandmother died the following March. The creels went to the Coyles, which is what her grandmother had arranged in February without telling anybody, because she was that sort of woman.

Maeve is twenty-six now and she has not seen the seal again, and she does not expect to, and she has never once gone looking.

She did tell her father, in the end. Not that summer — years later, in the van, going to Sligo for something else.

He drove for about a mile without saying anything.

Then he said: "Where was she?"

"Just off the black buoy."

"Mm," her father said. "That's where she was for me."

And he put the radio on, and they went on to Sligo, and that is the only conversation the two of them have ever had about it.
