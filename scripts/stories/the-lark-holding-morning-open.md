## How Long a Morning Is

Mornings at Fuentelosa were an hour and forty minutes long and mornings everywhere else were not, and Rocío did not find this out until she was eleven and went to stay with her cousins in Palencia.

She came back arguing about it and got nowhere for about a year.

The argument, as she put it at eleven, was that mornings in Palencia were over almost as soon as they had started. Her cousins got up, and had something, and were doing things, and by half past eight the whole apparatus of the day was running at full speed with the previous part of it having lasted, as far as Rocío could see, about twenty minutes.

Her aunt said this was because Palencia was a city and cities are busy.

Rocío said that Fuentelosa was not busy and the morning was longer, and that if being busy made mornings short then it should be the other way round.

Her aunt said she was being literal, which was the word Rocío was going to hear from four different adults over the following year, and which she came to understand meant *you are correct and I have no answer*.

The difficulty is that a morning is not a measurable thing. Everybody knows roughly what it means — the part between it getting light and the day properly starting — and nobody can put a number on where it ends, and every adult Rocío put the question to gave her a different answer and then said she was being literal.

What she eventually worked out, at twelve, with a notebook, was that she could not measure the morning but she could measure the lark.

The lark went up before it was light. That is normal; larks do that everywhere. She went up out of the barley on the north side of the road in the dark, and she climbed, and she sang the whole way, and at the top she hung there and went on singing.

It is worth saying what that is actually like, because most people have never heard it and assume it is pleasant background noise.

It is not background. A skylark in full song is loud — properly loud, audible across a kilometre of open ground — and it does not stop. There is no phrase and no pause and no repeat you can get hold of; it is a continuous rolling stream of sound with no shape to it whatsoever, going on and on from a bird you usually cannot see because she is two hundred metres up and the size of a thumb.

Rocío had heard it every morning of her life and had never once thought about it, in the way you do not think about the sound of your own house.

And then she came down and stopped.

At Fuentelosa the coming down happened an hour and forty minutes after the going up, give or take about four minutes, from March to July, and Rocío has the figures for three seasons in a school exercise book.

In Palencia — she took the notebook, she was that sort of child — the lark over the field behind her aunt's house went up in the dark and came down after fifty-five minutes.

Both of those are within the range of what larks do. She looked it up later and larks are variable and there is nothing whatsoever remarkable about either number.

The remarkable part is the other half of it, which took her longer to see and which cannot be put in a notebook.

She got to it sideways, from her grandmother, who said something in passing in the kitchen that Rocío did not understand for a fortnight.

Her grandmother said that in Palencia they had *nothing before the day*.

At Fuentelosa the day did not start until she came down.

## What the Morning Is For

An hour and forty minutes is a great deal of time and Fuentelosa used every minute of it, and everybody in that village would have told you they were simply getting on with things.

The bread came out at about the fortieth minute.

Rocío's grandmother had run those ovens since 1979 and could have told you the time by what stage the second batch was at, and she was, by some distance, the person in Fuentelosa who understood the morning best and thought about it least. Rocío's grandmother had been at the ovens since before the lark went up and the queue formed at about half past six and stood in the road for the better part of an hour, and the queue was not a queue in the sense of people waiting to buy bread. It was where the village found out what had happened.

Somebody's water. Somebody's roof. A tractor for sale. Whose son was back from Bilbao and for how long. The whole of it, every day, in the road, in the dark going grey, with the lark up above the barley and nobody in any hurry at all.

The old men were on the wall by the fountain from about the fiftieth minute. That is not a figure of speech either — Rocío timed them for a fortnight and it was between forty-eight and fifty-five, every day, four of them, and they sat there until the lark came down and then they got up and went and did something.

The school did not open until the ninetieth minute. It has done since 1974 and there is a memo about it in a drawer that gives a reason to do with the bus that has not been true since 1991.

And her mother and her aunt walked to the cemetery on Thursdays, which took an hour there and back with the standing about, and which fitted into the morning exactly and would not have fitted into a morning of fifty-five minutes, and which is a thing they had both done every Thursday for nineteen years without either of them ever describing it as anything.

None of this was arranged. That is what Rocío has never been able to get anybody to see. Nobody in Fuentelosa sat down in 1950 and allocated the morning. It had simply grown into the space, the way a plant grows into a pot, and the pot was an hour and forty minutes wide.

And because nobody had arranged it, nobody could see it. Every single one of those things had a reason that had nothing to do with the length of anything. The bread queue was long because people are friendly. The men were on the wall because they were old. The school opened late because of a bus in 1974.

Ask anybody in that village why the morning was the length it was and they would have looked at you as though you had asked why the road was the length it was.

## The Nineteenth of April

She did not sing on the nineteenth of April.

Rocío was awake for it, because she was awake for all of them that spring, and what she remembers is lying in bed at about a quarter to six waiting for the moment when the sound starts.

It did not start.

She lay there for a while telling herself it was late. Then she told herself she had missed the start, which was not possible, because she had been awake. Then she got up.

She got up at six and went out to the road and it was getting light and there was nothing in the sky at all.

The morning that followed was fifty minutes long.

Not because anybody decided anything. Nobody in that village knew what was wrong; they only knew that something was, in the way you know a room has been rearranged before you can say what has moved. What happened was that everybody did everything about forty minutes too fast.

The bread came out at the fortieth minute as always, and the queue formed, and the queue broke up after about ten minutes instead of fifty, and Rocío stood in the road and watched twenty-two people run out of things to say to each other for the first time in her life.

That is the image she has kept for twenty-two years and it is not a dramatic one. Nobody was upset. There was no silence and no awkwardness. People simply finished — they said the things there were to say, and there were about ten minutes' worth, and then they picked up their bread and went, one after another, entirely cheerfully, and the road was empty by twenty past.

The forty minutes that usually came after the ten were not full of anything important. That is the point. They were full of the second and third things — the ones you get to once the first ones are used up, the ones that start with somebody saying *anyway* — and nobody in Fuentelosa had ever noticed that those existed, because nobody had ever run out.

The old men sat down on the wall at the fiftieth minute and got up again at the fifty-eighth.

One of them, Don Aniceto, who was eighty-six, went home and went back to bed, which had not happened in the eleven years Rocío had been watching that wall, and which was discussed in the village for a fortnight and attributed to his hip.

The school opened on time, at the ninetieth minute by the clock, and by then the day had been going for so long that four children were already in the yard and the caretaker had been standing at the gate since twenty past.

And on the Thursday — because it went on, that is the part people forget, it went on — her mother and her aunt got to the cemetery and turned round at the gate and came back, and neither of them said why, and Rocío asked her mother about it eleven years later and her mother said she had no memory of it at all.

## The Strip

The barley on the north side of the road had been ploughed in the second week of March.

That is the whole explanation and it took Rocío four days and one telephone call to establish it. The field had changed hands in the winter. The new man was from Valladolid and was perfectly reasonable and was farming it in the ordinary modern way, which meant the whole field, up to the road, in one pass.

Larks nest on the ground. They need a strip of rough where nothing runs a machine over them between April and July, and if there is no strip there is no nest, and if there is no nest there is no bird going up before light and hanging there for an hour and forty minutes over a village that had built its entire morning underneath her.

Rocío has since read enough to know that this is one of the best-documented declines in Europe and that Fuentelosa's version of it was not special in any way. The birds have gone from most of that country for exactly this reason and there are papers about it and there is nothing supernatural in a single line of the mechanism.

What was special about Fuentelosa is only that somebody was counting.

Rocío was twelve and she took this to her grandmother, who took it to the wall by the fountain.

It was not a meeting and there was no vote. What there was, over about nine days, was a conversation that happened in the bread queue in the fifty minutes it had left, and at the end of the nine days three farmers on the north side of that road had each agreed to leave four metres unploughed along the top edge, which between them made a strip about six hundred metres long.

The man from Valladolid was one of the three and Rocío would like that recorded, because when she tells this story people always want him to be the villain and he was not. He asked what it was for. He was told, by her grandmother, in about two sentences, in a bread queue. He said all right.

Rocío asked her grandmother afterwards what the two sentences had been. Her grandmother could not remember and did not consider it interesting.

What Rocío thinks now, having spent twenty years in meetings, is that this is the part of the story nobody believes and the part most worth attending to. There was no campaign. Nobody wrote to anybody. Six hundred metres of a working field came out of production because an old woman said something to a man in a queue and he had no particular reason to say no.

There was a lark the following spring. There has been one every spring since.

Rocío is thirty-four. She works in Palencia and drives back at weekends, and Fuentelosa now has forty-one people in it and the school shut in 2016.

The bread queue is six people and it lasts about twenty minutes and there are two men on the wall.

But the morning is still an hour and forty minutes long, and on Saturdays Rocío gets up in the dark for no reason she can defend, and stands in the road, and waits for it to start.
