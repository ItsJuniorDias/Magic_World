## The Post

There is a post at the end of the inner wall at Porthallow that used to hold a light and has held nothing since 1988, and a cormorant sits on it.

The light was taken off when the new one went on the outer arm and the post was left because taking a post out of a Cornish harbour wall is a great deal more work than leaving it in. It is oak, about two metres proud, black, with four iron bolts in it that have wept rust down the stone for forty years.

Cormorants sit on posts. It is what they are for, more or less. They have wettable feathers, which is the trade they have made in exchange for being able to chase a fish underwater at a depth that would kill most birds, and it means that after a session in the water they have to get out and stand somewhere with their wings open like a wet umbrella until they are dry.

Every harbour in Britain has one on a post and nobody looks at it twice.

Merryn has since spent a career explaining to people that this is not a design fault. A bird that could stay dry would float too well, and a bird that floats too well cannot get down to forty metres and stay there for a minute, and the cormorant has traded comfort for a hunting depth that no other British seabird of its size gets anywhere near.

The wet umbrella on the post is not a bird recovering from a mistake. It is the running cost of being extremely good at something.

What Merryn's family will tell you about the one at Porthallow — and what her grandmother will tell you at length, and what her uncle will tell you shortly and while looking at something else — is that she is on that post at the start of the ebb, when the boats go, and she is on it when they come back.

And in between, if they are all in, she goes.

If they are not all in, she does not.

Nobody in Porthallow claims she is counting. Merryn wants that on the record because it is the version that gets repeated by visitors and it is not what anybody there actually says.

What her grandmother said, always in the same words, was: *she's not off yet.*

Merryn tested this properly at twelve because she had reached the age at which you test what your family tells you.

She did it for the whole of a summer with a notebook and a pair of binoculars from her bedroom window, which looks down the harbour, and the method was simple: count the boats out, count the boats in, and record whether the post was occupied at three-hourly intervals.

She was rigorous about the parts she could be rigorous about, which is a thing she is still proud of. She wrote the boat count before she looked at the post, every time, so that she could not adjust one to fit the other. She had a rule that a bird had to be on the post at the moment she looked and not within a minute of it.

She had no rule at all about how hard she looked, which is where the whole thing falls apart, and it took her until about nineteen to see it.

Fifty-eight days. Eleven days on which every boat was back within the usual window and the post was empty by an hour after. Forty-four days on which she could not get a clean count because of visitors, weather or her own laziness.

The forty-four is the honest number and it is the one she puts in first now. Doing anything properly for fifty-eight consecutive days at twelve turned out to be about four times harder than she had estimated, and the reason she remembers that summer at all is mostly that she failed at it.

And three days on which a boat was late, and on all three of those the post had a cormorant on it, and on two of the three that cormorant was still there in the dark.

The third one came in at half past four in the afternoon with a broken hydraulic and the post was empty by six.

## What Counting Would Mean

Merryn is thirty-one and she works in fisheries science and she will tell you exactly what is wrong with all of that.

Three is not a number. Fifty-eight days of a twelve-year-old with binoculars is not a study. There is no control, no blind, and an enormous confirmation bias, because on the days when she was expecting the post to be occupied she looked harder at it.

And there is a mechanism problem that she thinks is fatal and that nobody in Porthallow has ever engaged with.

A cormorant does not care about boats.

They are not a food source, they are not a threat, and the fleet at Porthallow is eleven small crabbers that go out at dawn and come back when they are done. A bird sitting on a post at the end of a wall is sitting there because it is a good post with a good view of the channel and it needs to dry, and it leaves when it is dry, and how long that takes depends on wind and sun and how long it has been in the water.

There is also no reason to think it is one bird. Merryn has tagged enough of them to know how much they move; the post at Porthallow is a good post and there are several hundred cormorants working that stretch of coast, and the idea that the same individual has been on that pole since her grandmother was a girl is not defensible for a second.

The days a boat is late are days with weather in them. Weather is also what keeps a cormorant on a post.

A wet bird in still cold air takes a long time to dry. A wet bird in a breeze is off in twenty minutes. So the confound is not incidental — it is the strongest single predictor of both variables, and any correlation between late boats and occupied posts falls straight out of it with nothing left over.

That is the whole of it. She has said it in exactly those words to her uncle, at a table, at nineteen, and her uncle said, "Right," and did not argue, and she has never once managed to make it stick.

## The Ninth of October

The *Marged Ann* went out at ten past six with two aboard and did not come back.

Merryn was fourteen. It was a flat grey day with no weather in it whatsoever, which matters, and the other ten boats were in by half past two.

She has checked the record. Wind at the Lizard that afternoon was force two to three, visibility good, sea slight. It is the single data point in the whole of her life that her own explanation does not cover, and she has been extremely careful never to build anything on it.

By four the harbour knew. By five the coastguard had it and there was a lifeboat out of Falmouth and two of the crabbers had gone back out, and by seven it was dark and there was a helicopter working the ground west of the Manacles.

Merryn was at her grandmother's, which is where the family goes.

There is a particular thing that happens in a village on an evening like that and it is not what people imagine. Nobody stood on the wall. Nobody wailed.

Merryn's cousin, who was seventeen, did the sandwiches. That is what he did for eleven hours: he made sandwiches for people who were not eating, and put them on the table, and took them away, and made more, and nobody told him to and nobody stopped him.

Merryn has thought about that as much as she has thought about the bird. What there was, for eleven hours, was a kitchen with too many people in it and a great deal of tea and long stretches of ordinary conversation that stopped every time a phone went.

At about nine her grandmother got up and went to the window and stood there for a while.

She was seventy-eight and she had buried a brother off that coast in 1969 and she had not said a word for two hours.

Merryn asked her what she was doing.

Her grandmother said: "She's on the post."

And Merryn, who had done fifty-eight days with a notebook two years earlier and knew exactly what she thought about all of it, went and stood at that window and looked down the harbour at a post in the dark with a bird-shaped thing on it and did not say one word about confirmation bias.

They found the *Marged Ann* at twenty to two in the morning, eleven miles south-south-west, with a fouled prop and a dead radio and two extremely cold and extremely embarrassed men aboard.

The prop had picked up a length of somebody else's rope, which happens perhaps twice a year in that fleet and is normally a nuisance and an hour with a knife. The radio had failed for a reason that was never satisfactorily established and that one of the two men was extremely defensive about for the next decade.

Nobody was hurt.

## What She Believes

The post was empty at first light.

Merryn has never known whether it was empty at three in the morning or at six, because by the time the boats came in past the wall she was asleep on a sofa with her boots on.

She is thirty-one. She has a doctorate. Her work is on cormorant foraging in the Western Approaches and she has, at the last count, put satellite tags on ninety-one birds, and there is nothing in the literature or in her own data that supports a single thing her grandmother said at that window.

She holds both of these positions and has stopped finding it difficult.

It took her about a decade. At nineteen she thought she had to choose, and chose the first, and was insufferable about it at two Christmases.

The first is that it is a bird on a post. Everything about that night is explained by an ordinary cormorant doing an ordinary thing at an ordinary time, in front of a family who needed something to look at.

The second is that on the ninth of October her grandmother got up and went to the window at nine o'clock at night and said four words, and every person in that kitchen — eleven of them, including two who had been at sea for forty years and did not believe in anything — stopped talking and looked, and then went back to the tea.

And that whatever that was, it held that room together for another five hours.

Merryn goes home about four times a year. Her grandmother died in 2021.

She always looks at the post. She has never seen a bird on it in the dark and she has been checking for seventeen years, which she is aware is exactly the kind of statement that a person makes when they have already decided.

Her uncle is seventy-one and still goes out, in the same boat, with a radio that works and a second radio in a bag.

She asked him once, at about twenty-five, when she was full of the tags and the data and the Western Approaches, whether he actually believed any of it about the post.

He thought about it for a good while, which he does.

Then he said that when he was a boy his own grandmother had told him the same thing, and that he had gone out for forty years knowing that if he was late there would be a bird on that post and somebody in a kitchen looking at it, and that he had never once needed to work out whether it was true.

"It's not about the bird," he said. "It's that somebody's counting."
