## The Map With No Roads

The hare kept an office in the root of a beech, which Marisol found by walking into it.

She had been going along the top edge of the wood where the bracken thins out, not paying attention, thinking about something else entirely, and her boot went into a gap between two roots and stopped against something that was not earth. It made a sound. Wood on wood. A small hollow knock, like knuckles on a door, which in a sense it was.

She got down on her knees and put her eye to the gap.

There was a room in there.

It was about the size of a bread oven and it had been fitted out with considerable care. There was a desk made from a slate roof tile balanced on two flints. There was a lamp that was not a lamp but a piece of foxfire in a nutshell, giving off that cold green light that rotten wood gives off in autumn. And there were papers — a great many papers, rolled and stacked in the corners and held down against the draught with acorns.

The hare was at the desk, drawing.

It was an ordinary hare in every respect that Marisol could see. Brown, long, with the black tips on the ears and the eye set high in the side of the head the way a hare's eye is, so that it can see behind itself without turning. It was holding something in one forepaw and moving it across the paper in short strokes.

It went on drawing for some time after she put her eye to the gap. Then it said, without looking up:

"You're standing in the light."

Marisol moved.

"Thank you."

She waited. It kept drawing. She was aware that she was kneeling in wet bracken and that her knees were getting cold and that she did not want to be anywhere else.

Eventually the hare put the stylus down and turned the paper round with both paws so that she could see it through the gap.

It was a map of her wood.

She knew it immediately, and it was a good map. There was the stream, with the two kinks in it near the bottom where it goes round the boulder. There was the fallen oak that had come down in the storm three winters ago and had been lying across the bottom path ever since, forcing everyone into a detour through the nettles. There was the fence, and the place where the fence gives up and just stops, halfway along, for no reason anyone has ever explained.

And there was a path.

It ran from the stile at the bottom corner, up along the flank of the hill, and over the top to the ridge. It was drawn firmly, as a path is drawn, with a dashed line and a little scale bar beside it: eight hundred metres.

Marisol had lived at the bottom of that wood her whole life.

"There isn't a path there," she said.

"No," the hare agreed.

"I've been here twelve years. There's no path there. There's bracken up to here and then a bank and then more bracken."

"Yes," said the hare. "It's a matter of timing."

## Following a Path Not Made

It explained with the particular patience of somebody who has explained the same thing many times and has stopped being irritated by it, which Marisol thought was probably a considerable achievement.

Most mapmakers, it said, draw what is there.

This is useful. Nobody is saying it is not useful. If you want to know where the stream is, a map of where the stream is will tell you where the stream is, and you can go and put your hand in it and confirm the matter.

But it is not interesting.

"I draw," said the hare, "what is going to be there."

Marisol asked how that was possible, which she was slightly ashamed of afterwards, because it was the obvious question and the hare had clearly been asked it a great many times.

The answer was not what she expected.

"Paths are not built," it said. "Almost nobody understands this. People think somebody decides — a council, a landowner, somebody with a plan — and then men come with gravel. That is a road. A road is built. A path is worn."

It got down off the stack of paper it had been sitting on and went to the wall of the office, where a larger map was pinned up with thorns.

"Somebody goes first," it said. "One person. And they go through the wrong part of the bracken, at the wrong time of year, usually for a bad reason. They have left something at the top. They are avoiding somebody on the road. They are twelve and they are in a temper. The reason is nearly always small and nearly always private."

"And then?"

"And then the bracken is bent. Only a little. But the next person who comes along that way — and there is always a next person, that is the whole trick of it — the next person finds that going where the bracken is bent is very slightly easier than going where it is not. And they are not deciding anything. They are not thinking about it at all. They are simply taking the easier of two options, which is what everything alive does, always."

It turned round.

"Do that four hundred times and you have a path. Do it four thousand times and somebody puts up a sign with the mileage on it and a little picture of a boot. And by then everyone has completely forgotten that there was a first person, and that the first person went that way by mistake."

Marisol sat back on her heels.

"So how do you know which way the first person will go?"

There was a pause.

"I don't," said the hare. "I guess."

"You guess?"

"I guess extremely well. I have been doing it for a long time and I understand where people are likely to leave a jumper. But it is a guess, and I have been wrong four hundred and six times, and I do not enjoy discussing it."

## Four Hundred and Six

She should not have asked about the four hundred and six.

She knew it at the time. The hare had said, very clearly, that it did not enjoy discussing them, and Marisol had heard that and had waited nearly a week, and then had asked anyway, because she was twelve and because a locked drawer is a locked drawer.

The hare did not refuse.

It went to the corner of the office where the oldest papers were — the ones gone soft and furred at the edges, held down with a flint instead of an acorn — and it pulled one out with its teeth and unrolled it across the slate with a paw at each corner.

"Nineteen forty-six," it said.

The map showed a stretch of country Marisol half recognised: the valley on the other side of the ridge, before the reservoir, when the reservoir was still a river and three farms. And there was a path on it, drawn firmly, dashed, with a scale bar. It went from a farm gate up over the shoulder of the hill to a chapel.

"That's a good path," said the hare. "That is one of the best I ever did. The gradient is right, it avoids the wet ground in February, it comes out at the chapel from the south so you get the light on the door. A person walking that path would have enjoyed it every single time."

"What happened?"

"Nothing happened. That is precisely the difficulty."

It sat back.

"The first person did not go. I had them — a woman at the farm, thirty-one, walked to the chapel on a Sunday and back, every week, and the road went round the long way and she resented it. Everything was in place. She had the reason and the temper and the legs." The hare's ears went flat and then came up again. "And in the March her sister got ill in Tarragona, and she went down to nurse her, and she did not come back. That is all. There is no more to it than that."

Marisol looked at the dashed line going up over the shoulder of the hill to the chapel with the light on the door.

"Couldn't somebody else have gone?"

"Somebody else did not have her reason. That is what people never understand about this." It turned the map round to look at it the right way up, which Marisol thought it probably did not need to do. "A path is not a good idea. A path is one person's bad afternoon, repeated by strangers who never knew about the afternoon. If you take away the afternoon, there is nothing left to repeat."

"So it's just — gone."

"It is under a dozen metres of water now, along with the farm and the chapel. But it was gone long before the reservoir. It was gone in the March of nineteen forty-six." It rolled the map up. "I keep them because somebody should. That is the entire reason and I have stopped looking for a better one."

Marisol sat back on her heels in the wet bracken.

"Doesn't that make you want to stop?"

The hare put the flint back on the roll.

"Four hundred and six," it said. "In two hundred and twelve years. Which means the other ones are out there being walked on, right now, this minute, by people who think the ground has always been like that." It went back to the desk. "You are standing in the light again."

She moved.

She thought about that conversation for a long time afterwards, and the part she came back to was not the woman who went to Tarragona. It was the arithmetic. Two hundred and twelve years, and it had counted the failures precisely and had not, as far as she could tell, counted the others at all.

## The Path That Made Itself

She walked it that afternoon.

Stile to ridge, the way the map had it, and it was awful.

The bracken came up past her shoulders in the middle stretch and it did not want her there. It closed behind her. It got down her collar. There was a bank two thirds of the way up that was not marked on the map at all and that she had to go up on her hands, and there was a wet patch in a dip near the middle where her boot went in to the ankle and came out with a noise like a kiss.

It took her an hour and ten minutes. The bottom path takes twenty.

She did it again on the Thursday, because she had left her jumper at the top.

That was all. That was the entire reason. She was cross about the jumper and she went back up the way she knew rather than the way that was sensible, and the second time it took fifty minutes because the bracken in the middle stretch was already bent.

In August her cousins came from the coast, three of them, and she took them up that way because she said it was the fastest route to the view.

That was a lie. She knew it was a lie when she said it. She has never entirely worked out why she said it — some part of her wanted them to go that way, and the reason was small and private, exactly as the hare had said it would be.

By the following spring there was a line of flattened green from the stile to the ridge, visible from the road if you knew to look.

By the spring after that there was bare earth showing in it in three places.

The year after that somebody put a plank across the wet part near the middle. Nobody knows who. Marisol asked four people and they all assumed it had been there for years.

She is grown up now, and she went back once, in her thirties, on a cold bright day in February when the bracken was down and everything was visible.

The path is a path. It has a sign at the bottom, on the stile, a small green one with the mileage and a little picture of a boot. It says 800m, which she thought was a nice touch, or would have been if anyone else had been there to say it to.

She went along the top edge of the wood afterwards, where the bracken thins, and she looked for a long time for a beech with a gap between two roots.

She found several beeches. She found several gaps.

She was not, in the end, entirely surprised, and she did not stay long, because the light was going and she had a train.
