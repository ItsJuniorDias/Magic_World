## Older Than the Wall

The harbour wall at Kalamáki was finished in 1961 and the turtle was already old, and this is not a family legend — it is in a photograph in the town hall.

The photograph is of the opening. There are forty or fifty people on the new mole in good clothes and there is a priest and a man from the ministry and a boy on the parapet who is now eighty-two and runs the hardware shop. And in the bottom right of the frame, in the shallows inside the arm of the wall, there is a shape.

Nobody who has not lived there sees it. Ela's grandmother had pointed it out to her at about six with a fingernail on the glass, and once you have seen it you cannot see the photograph any other way: the flat oval, the notch out of the back edge of the shell, the head just under.

The notch is the point. It is a piece missing from the rear margin of the carapace, about the width of two fingers, with a smooth healed edge, and the turtle on the beach at Kalamáki in the summer of 2026 has it in exactly the same place.

Ela is twelve and has been told that this is not proof of anything and that the sea is full of damaged turtles.

She has heard that from four adults and it is perfectly true and she has never believed it for a second, and she has the photograph on her phone at a resolution that the town hall did not authorise.

The argument against her is good and she can make it better than any of the adults who made it to her.

Loggerheads take damage constantly. They get hit by propellers and caught in gear and bitten, and a healed notch in the rear margin is one of the commonest injuries there is. On a beach that gets between four and eleven nesting females a year, over sixty years, you would expect a great many notched turtles and there is no reason at all to think that two photographs sixty-five years apart show the same animal.

What Ela has against that is the position of the notch, which is the same to within about a centimetre, and the fact that nobody in Kalamáki has ever, in her lifetime or her grandmother's, reported a second notched turtle.

She is aware that this is not evidence. She would like it noted that she has never once claimed it was.

A loggerhead can live somewhere in the region of sixty or seventy years and possibly a great deal longer; nobody knows, because nobody has ever followed one all the way through. If the turtle in the 1961 photograph was breeding then, she was at least twenty-five, which puts her hatching somewhere in the middle 1930s, which means she came out of that sand before the road, before the electricity, before the wall, and before every single person now living in that town.

The town knows this. It is not a secret and it is not particularly discussed. She is *i grià* — the old woman — and she comes up the beach for about six nights in July, and everybody leaves her alone, and the tavernas turn their sea-facing lights off in July because they always have.

The light business is worth explaining because visitors find it inconvenient and complain about it in reviews.

Hatchlings come out at night and they go towards the brightest thing on the horizon, which for the whole history of the species has been the sea reflecting the sky. A lit taverna forty metres inland is brighter, and they go up the beach instead of down it, and they die in the road.

Nobody in Kalamáki has ever needed that explained. The tavernas were turning their seaward lights off in July before there was a word for it and before there was a law about it, and the law, when it came in 2010, changed nothing whatsoever in that town.

## The Extension

The plan for the extension came to the town in the February and it was, everybody agreed, a good plan.

The harbour needed it. The wall of 1961 was built for wooden boats and the fishing fleet was gone; what came in now were ferries in the summer and yachts, and both wanted deeper water and a longer arm, and there was money from the region for exactly this and the money had a date on it.

The proposal took the arm out a further hundred and forty metres and turned it about fifteen degrees south.

Ela's father had the drawing on the table for a week and was in favour, and so was almost everybody, and Ela would like that stated clearly because the story is not about a town resisting a development. The town wanted it. Her own family wanted it. Two of her cousins had left for Athens in the previous four years and the ferry berth was the only argument anybody had for them coming back.

The consultation was held in the school hall in March and Ela went with her grandmother because her grandmother went to everything.

The engineer was a woman from Patras called Despina Vlachou and Ela would like it on the record that she was excellent. She had drawings. She answered every question, including four that were rude. When the man from the hardware shop asked about the sand she did not brush him off; she put up a slide with three years of survey data and explained longshore drift for six minutes to a room of eighty people, most of whom had known about longshore drift their entire lives without having a word for it.

And then somebody at the back said: "What about the turtles?"

And Despina Vlachou said that this had been considered, and put up the environmental assessment, and it was a real assessment and it was not a box-ticking exercise. The nesting beach was protected. The works were outside the season. There was a monitoring condition.

She said that the assessment showed no nesting within the affected zone.

There was a particular quality of silence in that hall which Ela has thought about a great many times since, because at twelve she could feel it and could not read it. About eighty people knew something and not one of them stood up.

Ela looked round the hall. She saw the man from the hardware shop, who is in the 1961 photograph, look at the floor. She saw her grandmother's hands on the back of the chair in front of her. She saw two women from the church turn very slightly towards each other and not say anything.

Then the meeting moved on to parking, and the parking took forty minutes.

## What the Assessment Had

Ela's grandmother explained it on the walk home in about four sentences.

The assessment was not wrong. It had three years of data — 2023, 2024, 2025 — from the national monitoring programme, which is a real programme run by serious people, and in those three years there had been no nest in the affected zone. That is what the survey found and that is what the survey said.

The old woman had not nested in the affected zone since 2019.

She had nested there in 2019, and 2016, and 2015, and 2011, and in a great many years before that, and she moves up and down about two hundred metres of that beach in a way that nobody has ever charted because nobody has ever been paid to chart it.

"So why didn't anyone say?"

Her grandmother walked for a while.

"Because what would we say? *We remember?* That woman had papers, Ela. She had three years of counts done by people with clipboards. If eleven of us stand up in a hall and say we recall a turtle being further along in about 2015, what is she supposed to do with that? She'd be polite and she'd write it down and it would go nowhere, and she'd be right, because we would be eleven people remembering."

Ela said that they would not be wrong.

"No," her grandmother said. "We would be right and useless. Those are not opposites."

Ela has thought about that sentence more than about anything else anybody said to her that year.

She said, on the road up from the school, that there had to be a way of turning one into the other.

Her grandmother said that there was, and that it was called evidence, and that the reason a town full of people who had watched a beach for sixty years had none of it was that not one of them had ever written anything down.

## Sixty Years of Somebody's Holidays

The thing Ela did next took her from March to July and it was not clever, it was only tedious.

She asked for photographs.

She started with her own family, which produced a shoebox and two albums and about four hundred pictures of that beach going back to 1968, because her great-grandfather had bought a camera and every generation since had photographed the same three hundred metres of sand every August.

Then she asked the hardware shop. Then the two tavernas. Then, because her grandmother made her, the church, which had a folder of parish outings.

Then she put a sheet up in the shop asking anybody with old beach photographs to bring them in, and her mother told her nobody would, and about sixty people did.

They came in over about five weeks, in envelopes and in biscuit tins and on two occasions in a plastic bag, and eleven of them arrived with the person standing there wanting to explain the photograph, which added considerably to the time and which Ela has since decided was the best part of the whole summer.

An old man called Stamatis brought four pictures of a boat and stayed for two hours. A woman Ela had never spoken to brought her father's slides and a projector and they set it up against the shop wall after closing.

What she was looking for, in every single photograph, was a turtle track.

A loggerhead track is unmistakable and it does not wash out for a day or two: two parallel lines of flipper marks with a drag between them, going up the beach and coming back, wide as a small car. In a holiday snapshot of children with a ball, if there was a track that morning, it is in the background whether anybody meant it or not.

She found ninety-one of them.

It took her until the middle of July and she did it on a kitchen table with a ruler and the church tower and the corner of the hardware shop as fixed reference points, and she plotted every one onto a printout of the harbour survey drawing.

The method is the part she is proudest of and it was not hers; it came from a video about photogrammetry that she watched about nine times.

If you can see two fixed things in a photograph — and on that beach you can nearly always see the church tower and the corner of the hardware shop, because they are the only two tall things — then you can work out roughly where the camera was and roughly where anything in the frame sits along the sand. It is not accurate. She reckoned her error at about fifteen metres and she said so in the email, twice.

Forty-four of the ninety-one were inside the affected zone.

They were spread across fifty-eight years. The most recent was 2019. The oldest was a black-and-white picture of two women in swimming costumes taken in July 1964, in which, behind them, going up the sand at the exact point where the new arm was to turn south, there is a track.

Ela sent the whole thing to Despina Vlachou in an email that her grandmother made her rewrite twice.

The reply came in eleven days and it was four paragraphs long and the first sentence was: *Thank you — this is the first long-term dataset anybody has offered me for this beach.*

The arm was built. It went out a hundred and forty metres, because the harbour needed it and because the money had a date on it.

It turns nine degrees south instead of fifteen, and the toe of it sits fifteen metres further out than the March drawing, and the difference costs about six per cent and was signed off in the September.

Ela is nineteen and studying in Athens and she is not studying engineering, which surprises people.

The old woman came up on the fourth of July last year, at about one in the morning, three hundred metres along, well outside everything, and the notch is still there.
