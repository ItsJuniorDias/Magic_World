## The Wood's Own Time

The Bois de Herbeuval ran seven minutes behind the rest of Belgium and had done since 1944, and Sidonie grew up assuming this was normal, in the way that children assume whatever is around them.

It is not a figure of speech and it is not about clocks. There is no clock in that wood.

What there is is a toad, under the third stone up from the ford, and everything in that wood takes its time from him.

Sidonie has looked at him perhaps thirty times in her life and has never once found him interesting to look at. He is a common toad, *Bufo bufo*, of the size and colour and general demeanour of every other common toad in the Ardennes. If you turned that stone over on a wet evening you would think nothing of him whatsoever, and every visiting biologist who has ever been shown him has thought nothing of him, and two of them have said so out loud in a way that Sidonie's grandfather never entirely forgave.

She had it explained to her at about six by her grandfather, who was the forester there for thirty-one years, and who explained it the way he explained the difference between beech and hornbeam — patiently, once, on the assumption that she would remember.

Dusk in a wood is not a time. It is an event. The birds go down in an order — blackbird, then robin, then wren — and the order is fixed and the intervals are fixed and every wood in Europe does it. The deer come out. The bats come off the roof of the old sawmill. The moths that come to the lime trees come, and they come within about ninety seconds of each other, every night, all summer.

All of that is triggered by light, in the books.

And the books are right, and Sidonie would like that on the record before anything else, because she has a diploma and she is not going to spend it on this. Light level drops below a threshold; the blackbird's eye reports it; the blackbird goes. It has been measured a thousand times in a thousand woods and there is no mystery in it at all.

In the Bois de Herbeuval it is triggered by him.

You can watch it happen if you know where to stand. There is a moment — a small dry sound, not a croak, more like a stone dropped in leaf litter — and about four seconds after it the blackbird goes, and then the whole thing unwinds down the valley in order, and the wood puts itself to bed.

The four seconds is the part that undoes people.

Sound travels. If the blackbird is two hundred metres up the valley it should hear the sound about six-tenths of a second after it is made, and if it is four hundred metres away it should be later still. It is not. Sidonie's grandfather put two people with two watches at two distances on eleven separate evenings in the summer of 1978 and the delay was four seconds at both, and he did it again in 1981 with three people because he did not believe himself the first time.

Sidonie's grandfather had timed it against a wristwatch on and off for thirty years and had it written in the back of a notebook, and the notebook is where the seven minutes comes from.

## Seven Minutes

The wood's dusk is seven minutes later than it should be for that latitude, that longitude, and that date.

It is not seven minutes later than sunset — that would be normal; every wood is later than sunset by some amount that depends on the canopy. It is seven minutes later than *itself*. Her grandfather had a hundred and forty pages of it, going back to when he took the job in 1961, and the whole of it is consistent to within about forty seconds, and the whole of it sits seven minutes behind the tables.

Forty seconds of scatter over sixty years is, in Sidonie's professional opinion, the most impressive thing in that notebook. Nothing biological is that consistent. Her own measurements since 2019 sit inside the same band and she has far better equipment than he ever had.

He had not discovered this. That was the part that took Sidonie longest to get out of him.

He had gone to the man who had the job before him, a man called Pirlot, in about 1963, with two years of measurements and a great deal of excitement, and Pirlot had listened to the whole thing in his kitchen and then said: "Oui. Sept minutes."

And that was all he said, and he changed the subject, and he was not rude about it, and he never discussed it again in the eleven years before he died.

Sidonie's grandfather described that kitchen to her once and the description has stayed with her. Pirlot at the table. The two years of figures spread out on it. An old forester looking at the work of a young one, recognising every column of it, and saying two words.

Her grandfather asked twice more over those years and got the same nothing.

So he did what anyone would do, which was to ask around, carefully, in a village where his family had not been for four generations and where he was therefore permanently new. And what he found was that everybody over the age of about forty knew, that nobody would say how they knew, and that the subject was not exactly forbidden — it was worse than forbidden, it was *declined*. People would go quiet, and be pleasant, and start talking about something else with real warmth, and there was nothing in it you could take offence at and no way at all to get past it.

He gave up in about 1971.

Not on the measurements. He kept those up to the week he retired and for eleven years after it. He gave up on the asking, which is a different thing, and Sidonie thinks the difference between those two is most of what she knows about him.

Sidonie did not give up, because she was twelve and because he told her about it in the summer she was twelve, which he has since admitted was not entirely an accident.

## Madame Grandjean

She got it out of Madame Grandjean in the September, and she got it by doing the one thing thirty years of adults had not done, which was to ask a question about something else.

Madame Grandjean was ninety-three and had been eleven in 1944 and had lived in the last house before the ford her entire life. Sidonie went down there on a Wednesday with a school project about the war that was real, that she had actually been set, and that she had chosen deliberately.

She did not ask about the toad. She asked about the wood.

This was not cunning. She had thought about it for a fortnight and arrived at it honestly: every adult in that village had spent sixty years declining one specific question, and it had not occurred to any of them, or to her grandfather, that the question was the wrong shape.

And Madame Grandjean, who had declined the other question from four generations of foresters, answered the one she was actually asked, and talked for an hour and ten minutes.

There had been people in that wood in the September of 1944. Not soldiers — a family, and then two families, from Marche, in the four days when the front went through and nobody in that part of the Ardennes knew from one hour to the next whose road it was. They were in the old sawmill, which was a ruin then as it is now, and there were nine of them and four were children.

The village fed them. That is how Madame Grandjean, at eleven, was involved: she carried a basket down the track past the ford, in the evening, because a child with a basket is a child with a basket.

You went at dusk. That was the arrangement and it was not negotiable — not in the light, because you would be seen, and not in the dark, because you could not find the sawmill in the dark and a torch was out of the question.

Madame Grandjean described the basket in detail — the cloth over it, the two jars, the weight of it on a child's arm at the point where the track goes up — and Sidonie understood that she had described that basket to herself a great many times over eighty years.

Dusk is about twenty minutes long and you had to be back over the ford before it closed.

"And on the third evening," said Madame Grandjean, "there was a patrol on the Bastogne road."

Sidonie did not write anything down.

"I was on the track. I heard them and I got into the ditch by the hornbeam and I lay there and I could not go on and I could not go back, and it was going dark, and my mother did not know where I was."

She said that she had lain in that ditch for a long time.

She said that when she finally got up and went the rest of the way, it was still just light enough to find the sawmill, and that it should not have been, and that she had known it should not have been even at eleven, because she had walked that track at dusk every evening of her life.

"How long?"

"About seven minutes," said Madame Grandjean.

## Nobody Mentions It

Sidonie went home and did not tell her grandfather for two days, and when she did tell him he sat at the table for a while and then went out to the shed and did not come back in for an hour.

Here is what neither of them has ever been able to establish.

Whether the toad did anything. Whether an eleven-year-old in a ditch in September 1944 was right about seven minutes, or whether she was a frightened child with no watch who has had eighty years to make the number tidy. Whether a wood can run late. Whether the whole of it is a hundred and forty pages of one careful man's measurements against tables that were, in fact, slightly wrong for that valley all along, which is the explanation Sidonie's mother offers and which nobody in the family has ever been able to knock over.

What they did establish is why nobody would say.

It is not superstition and it is not shame. Sidonie worked it out on the walk home from Madame Grandjean's and she has never had a better version of it.

If you say out loud that the wood is seven minutes slow, then the next thing anybody says is: *shouldn't it be put right?*

And there is no way to answer that question. There is no mechanism for putting it right, and there is nobody to ask, and there is a toad under the third stone up from the ford who has done one thing, once, extremely well, at a moment when it counted more than anything else that happened in that valley that year — and who has apparently not given the seven minutes back, and who is not going to, and who has never once been consulted about any of it.

So they leave it.

Sidonie has tested this on herself more than once and it holds. Every time she gets as far as imagining saying it out loud to somebody at the village hall, the next sentence in her head is *and what are we going to do about it* — and there is nothing to do about it, and the only thing that can happen is that a valley which has got on perfectly well for eighty years starts having a difficult conversation with itself about a toad.

Every forester of the Bois de Herbeuval since Pirlot has known and has said nothing, and the notebook has been handed on twice now and has a hundred and sixty pages, and the whole of it still sits seven minutes behind the tables.

Sidonie is the forester there now. She took it in 2019.

She measures it in May and September, which is more than her grandfather did, and she writes it in the same notebook in a hand nothing like his.

She has never mentioned it to anybody in the village, and last summer a boy from the last house before the ford asked her a very direct question at the edge of the wood, and she went quiet, and was pleasant, and started talking about something else with real warmth.

She heard herself do it and she has thought about it since.

She is fairly sure she will tell him. Not this year.
