## The Shape of the Points

Her grandmother read the stag the way other people read a barometer, which is to say quickly, without ceremony, and while doing something else.

They would be walking the top field in April and the stag would be at the far edge of the birches — he was always at the far edge of something, never in the open, never hidden — and her grandmother would glance over, and keep walking, and say something like *wet June* or *the pears will do nothing* and not mention it again.

Petra spent two summers assuming this was a joke that adults did.

She stopped assuming that in the August she was ten, when her grandmother looked at him across the stream in the second week of the month and stopped walking, which she never did, and stood there for a while.

"What?"

"He's put on a fourth on the left."

"So?"

Her grandmother started walking again. "So bring the ladder round on Thursday and we'll get the apples."

They got the apples on the Thursday and Friday, all of them, three weeks earlier than any year Petra could remember, and everybody in the house complained about the work and about the apples being hard.

Her mother complained the loudest and complained for two days, and the complaint had a specific shape to it that Petra only understood later: it was not about the apples. It was about being the household that did the strange thing, again, in a village where everybody could see the ladders from the road.

On the Sunday night the wind came in off the sea and took a dozen trees down along the river and stripped every orchard in the valley that had not been picked.

## Learning the Reading

Petra asked to be taught and her grandmother said no.

Not unkindly. She said: "I can't teach it because nobody taught me. I watched, and I got it wrong for about nine years, and then one year I got it right and I couldn't tell you what changed."

"That's not helpful."

"It isn't. I know." Her grandmother was doing something at the sink. "But if I make you a chart you'll read the chart and not the stag, and the chart will be wrong in a year that matters. So no."

What she did instead was better and much slower.

It was also, Petra realised at about thirty, the only way anybody has ever taught anybody anything difficult, and it is the method that no school has time for.

She started taking Petra with her. That was all. Three or four times a week, from April, they went up the top field or along the birches or down to the stream, and if the stag was there they looked at him, and Petra was asked one question.

*What's changed.*

Not what does it mean. Just what has changed.

For the first month Petra could not answer at all. A stag is a stag; the antlers are complicated and they are also moving because he is moving, and they are usually a hundred metres away against a background of branches which are also complicated.

By June she could say *he's grown*.

This was received without comment, which was the system. There was never any praise and there was never any correction. Her grandmother would hear the answer, and nod, or not nod, and they would keep walking, and Petra would spend the next four days working out which it had been.

She learned things in a strange order. She learned the light before she learned the antlers — that he was easiest to see in the hour before the sun went behind the ridge, and almost impossible at noon, and that this was not about brightness but about which way the shadows ran in the birches.

She learned that he had a place he stood and three places he might stand and that if he was in none of the four you would not find him that day and should go home.

She learned, in July, that he knew perfectly well they were there. That was the one that took her a while. He never fled and never approached and never fed while they were watching, and once, in the second week of July, he turned his head and looked directly at her grandmother for a period that Petra found extremely uncomfortable and that her grandmother met without any reaction at all.

"Does he know you?"

"He knows there's two of us and there used to be one."

By the end of July she could say *the right one's gone out and the left one's gone up*, which her grandmother received with a nod and no comment whatsoever.

By the following March — because it takes a year, and the antlers drop in the spring and grow again from nothing between April and August, and you cannot learn this thing in a season for the simple reason that there is only one of them per year — by the following March, Petra could look at a stag at ninety metres in poor light and say what was different from the week before.

The winter in between had been the hard part, and nobody had warned her about it.

The antlers come off in the spring. She had known this in the way you know a fact. She had not understood that it meant the stag she had spent five months learning would, in April, simply stop being the same object — that the whole structure she had been watching grow would drop off in a wood somewhere and a new one would start from two bare stumps.

She took it badly. She said something at the kitchen table about all of it having been a waste, and her grandmother let her finish and then said:

"You didn't learn the antlers. You learnt to look."

"That's a thing adults say."

"It is," her grandmother agreed. "It's also true, which is why they say it. Go up on Thursday and tell me you can't find him against those birches in nine seconds flat. You couldn't do that in April."

Petra went up on the Thursday partly to prove her wrong.

She found him in about four seconds, in flat grey light, at over a hundred metres, standing in the third of his four places, and she stood there in the cold feeling extremely annoyed about it.

She still could not say what any of it meant.

"That's fine," said her grandmother. "That's the whole of the first part and it takes most people three years."

## The Argument About Method

The trouble started when Petra tried to make it make sense.

She was thirteen by then and had discovered graphs, and she did what any reasonable person does, which was to write everything down.

She kept a book. Date, sketch, number of points on each side, and then, at the end of the season, what the weather had actually done. She sketched him every week from April to August for three years and she had a system of measurement involving a pencil held at arm's length that she was very pleased with.

At the end of the third year she had thirty-nine pages and she sat down to find the rule.

There was no rule.

She checked it four times over the fortnight, each time convinced she had made an arithmetic error, and each time she had not.

Or rather — and this took her a bad fortnight to accept — there were about nine rules, each of which worked for two years out of three, and none of which worked together, and several of which contradicted each other in ways that were not resolvable.

A fourth point on the left had meant an early hard autumn in the year of the apples. It had also appeared in a year when the autumn was long and soft and nothing happened at all.

She took the book to her grandmother in a temper.

The temper had been building for a fortnight and it was not really about the book.

It was about the fact that she had done everything properly. She had been rigorous. She had measured, and dated, and controlled for what you control for, and she had not fudged a single entry even when she badly wanted to. She had done, as far as she could tell, exactly what a person is supposed to do with a phenomenon.

And it had produced nothing. Not a partial rule, not a rule with exceptions — nothing at all. Three years of Thursdays.

Her grandmother read it properly, front to back, which took her most of an evening, and she was complimentary about the drawings.

Then she said: "You've written down the points."

"Yes."

"I've never once counted the points."

Petra said that the points were the only thing there was to count.

"I know," said her grandmother. "That's why you counted them."

She turned a page of the book, back near the start, to the second summer.

"Look at this one. April, you've got him at eight points and you've written *nothing yet*. And here's the same week the year after, eight points again, and you've written *nothing yet*." She tapped it. "Two identical entries. Do you remember those two springs?"

Petra did, faintly. One had been the year of the late frost that took the blossom. The other had been the year of nothing at all.

"So they weren't the same," said her grandmother. "They were the same number. Those are different words."

"Counting," her grandmother said, "is what you do when you can't see the shape yet. That's not a criticism. I counted for nine years." She closed the book and gave it back. "What I look at now is whether he looks like he's expecting something. And I can't draw that and I can't measure it and if I say it out loud in the village they'll think I've gone, so I don't."

## The Year He Didn't Change

The stag died in the November Petra was sixteen, of being old, on the far side of the birches, and there was another one by the following spring, which is how it goes.

Her grandmother died in the February after that.

Petra did not go up the top field at all that April, or in May, and by June she had convinced herself that she had lost the thing entirely — that whatever it was had been her grandmother's, and had been the sort of knowledge that is really a relationship, and that it had gone where she went.

She went up in the last week of July because her mother made her, on an errand about a gate.

The new stag was at the far edge of the birches. He was smaller, and darker, and he was standing with his head slightly turned, and he had a fourth on the left.

Petra stopped, exactly the way her grandmother had stopped in the second week of August six years earlier, and stood there in the top field and looked at him for a very long time.

She has never been able to say what she saw.

Not the fourth point; the fourth point was just the thing her eyes landed on. What she saw, and what she has never been able to put into a sentence that survives being said out loud, was that he looked like something was coming.

She went down and told her mother to get the apples in.

Her mother, who was not sentimental and who had spent thirty years mildly embarrassed about her own mother and the stag, looked at Petra for a second and then went and got the ladder.

The wind came in on the eighth of August.

Petra is forty-one now and she keeps the top field.

The field is not worth keeping, agriculturally. Three people have told her so, one of them with a spreadsheet. She has never written another thing down and she has never taught anybody, and she has an eleven-year-old nephew who comes up with her three or four times a week from April, and who is asked one question, and who has been getting more and more annoyed about it since Easter.

He asked her last Thursday what the point of the question was and she said that she could not tell him, and he said that was not an answer, and Petra said no, it was not, and kept walking.
